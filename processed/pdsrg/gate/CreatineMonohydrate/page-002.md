Mechanisms of Action
The predominate food sources of creatine are animal muscle meats, adding to the impractical ability to use foods to
saturate muscle creatine stores for the goal of increasing performance, size, or other known potential
supplementation benefits.25 PCr, with its high-energy phosphoryl transfer potential, serves to maintain intracellular
adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels.20 At rest, concentrations of ATP, PCr, and Cr in skeletal muscle are 4, 25, 13 mM,
respectively.5,6 During exercise, levels of ATP decline very little until stores of PCr are used.21 Since creatine
supplementation has been shown to increase intracellular levels of PCr, intracellular levels of ATP may be maintained
at higher levels for longer periods of time.7,22,23 Therefore, the aim of creatine loading with supplementation is much
like the goal of carbohydrate loading by endurance athletes, but instead of increasing glycogen storage, and thus
delaying glycogen depletion, loading creatine would enhance PCr levels and delay its depletion while accelerating
repletion. This practice would primarily benefit activities that are dependent on PCr as an energy source such as
sprinting and weightlifting. However, due to the ability of creatine to increase the muscle buffering capacity
(neutralizing lactic acid), creatine supplementation may also help support the glycolytic energy system thus offer
greater overall energy potential to athletes in sports other than just those involved in high intensity activities that
continuously stop and start.15,24 To be sure, the majority of studies focusing on creatine supplementation report an
increase in the body’s creatine pool,25,26,27,28 with exercise increasing the uptake and leading to an increase in exercise
performance during anaerobic activities.25,28 Early research by Volek et al.29 attributed the increases in strength
performance, after 12 weeks of creatine supplementation with exercise, to an increased total creatine pool resulting
in more rapid ATP regeneration between exercise sets, allowing athletes to maintain a greater training intensity and
improve the quality of the workouts throughout the entire training period. Additionally, creatine supplementation has
been shown to cause a reduction in plasma concentrations of hypoxanthine and lactate following exercise, suggesting
lower levels of anaerobic glycolysis, another possible contribution to delaying muscular fatigue by attenuating the
exercise induced decrease in muscle pH.30 Because of creatine’s aforementioned functions in the body (see Figure 1),
supplementing creatine to maximize stores thus strength, size, and/or performance, has become one of the most
popular and heavily researched nutritional supplement protocols.25,31,32,33,34
Creatine supplementation (CS) with exercise clearly improves many training outcomes such as activity specific physical
performance and skeletal muscle size and development,25,32,33,35,36,37,38 through at least one obvious mechanism of
action described above. The ability of CS to raise the muscle content of PCr significantly increases anaerobic capacity
and can lead to improved training bouts that can build on each other.26,27,28,29,39 CS may also work through other
unique muscle building/recovery/development mechanisms of action during the supplementing phase.26,40,41 These
more recent elucidated mechanisms of CS may include changes in gene expression,40,42 satellite cell proliferation,
insulin-like growth factor signaling,27 increase in growth hormone,41 alterations in myogenic transcription factors
leading to a reduction in serum myostatin (muscle growth inhibitor),43 improved neuromuscular function (facilitating
the reuptake of Ca2+ into sarcoplasmic reticulum),44 and as mentioned above,30 reduced exercise induced blood
lactate.45 Finally, CS may also participate in reducing muscle damage from high intensity resistance training and
endurance exercise.46
