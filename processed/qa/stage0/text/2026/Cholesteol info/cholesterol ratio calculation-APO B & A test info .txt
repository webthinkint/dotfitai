A cholesterol ratio is a simple calculation used to estimate cardiovascular risk by comparing different types of cholesterol in the blood. There are two common ratios clinicians and labs refer to:

1. Total Cholesterol : HDL Ratio (most common)
Formula:


Example:
Total Cholesterol = 200 mg/dL
HDL = 50 mg/dL


How to interpret it
Optimal: < 3.5
Average risk: ~ 4.0–5.0
Higher risk: > 5.0
👉 Lower is better, because HDL is protective.

2. LDL : HDL Ratio (often used by lipid specialists)
Formula:


Example:
LDL = 120 mg/dL
HDL = 60 mg/dL


Interpretation
Optimal: < 2.0
Acceptable: 2.0–3.0
Higher risk: > 3.0

3. Non-HDL Cholesterol (related but not a ratio)
Not technically a ratio, but very important clinically.

This captures all atherogenic particles (LDL, VLDL, remnants).

Why ratios matter (trainer & client friendly)
Ratios account for protective HDL, not just “high or low cholesterol”
Two people with the same LDL can have very different risk
Ratios often correlate better with insulin resistance and ApoB burden than LDL alone

Quick takeaway (one-liner)
Cholesterol ratio = Total Cholesterol ÷ HDL, and the lower the number, the better the risk profile—especially when paired with healthy triglycerides and ApoB.

There are two types of apoB: apoB-48, which is made in your gut, and apoB-100, which is made in your liver. ApoB-48 carries fat and cholesterol to your liver, where it is repackaged and combined with apoB-100. From here, it moves throughout your body.
The apoB protein attaches to receptors on your cells, which allows the lipoprotein, or bad cholesterol, into the cell. Once inside, it’s broken down, releasing the fat and cholesterol into your blood. The bad cholesterol can cause plaque buildup in your blood vessels.


Lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) is a fat-protein particle in your blood, similar to LDL ("bad" cholesterol) but more dangerous, that carries cholesterol; high levels, often genetic, significantly increase your risk for heart attack, stroke, and plaque buildup in arteries, acting as an independent risk factor even with normal cholesterol. It's inherited, usually doesn't change with diet/exercise, and is linked to inflammation and blood clots, making it a crucial but often overlooked heart health marker. 
(Lp(a) levels cannot be controlled by healthy eating and exercising. Lipoprotein apheresis (see below -blood filtering) is the only therapy approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treating high Lp(a) levels, and it is only approved for people with Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) who have LDL≥100 mg/dL, Lp(a)≥60 mg/dL, and coronary or other artery disease.Sep 10, 2025)
What it is
A type of lipoprotein (fat + protein) that transports fats through the blood. 
It contains apolipoprotein(a) [apo(a)] and apolipoprotein B (apoB-100). 
It's similar to LDL but unique, and high levels signal higher cardiovascular risk. 
Why high levels matter (Cardiovascular Risk)
Plaque: Promotes cholesterol deposit in artery walls (atherosclerosis).
Clotting: Makes blood clots more likely and harder to break down. 
Independent Risk: Increases heart attack/stroke risk regardless of other factors like LDL or blood pressure. 
Associated Conditions: Linked to heart valve disease (aortic stenosis) and irregular heart rhythms. 
Key characteristics
Genetically Determined: Levels are set by your genes, often by age 5, and aren't usually affected by lifestyle.
Prevalence: About 1 in 5 people have elevated levels. 
What to do
Get Tested: Ask your doctor for a blood test to measure Lp(a). 
Manage Other Risks: Aggressively control other heart disease factors (diet, exercise, blood pressure). 
Medication: Statins don't significantly affect Lp(a) levels, but newer treatments (like PCSK9 inhibitors) might lower it, though outcomes aren't fully established. 
Family: Due to its strong genetic link, family screening is recommended.

Therapy 
What to do Lipoprotein apheresis is a non-surgical medical treatment that physically filters harmful LDL cholesterol and Lp(a) from the blood for people with severe genetic cholesterol disorders (like Familial Hypercholesterolemia) who don't respond to diet or medications, involving blood drawn, filtered by a machine to remove bad fats, and returned, typically every two weeks for 2-4 hours, significantly reducing cardiovascular risk.  
How it Works
Blood Drawn: Blood is taken from a vein (like in the arm) and goes into a machine. 
Separation: The machine separates the plasma (liquid part) from blood cells. 
Filtration: The plasma is passed through a special filter (like dextran sulfate) that captures and removes LDL, Lp(a), and other pro-atherogenic particles. 
Blood Returned: The cleaned blood cells and filtered plasma are returned to the body. 
Who Needs It
Patients with Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) or severe genetic lipid disorders. 
Those with very high LDL or Lp(a) levels (e.g., >200 mg/dL LDL or >60 mg/dL Lp(a) with existing heart disease). 
When maximum doses of statins and other drugs fail. 
Key Benefits & Risks
Reduces Cardiovascular Events: Lowers heart attacks, strokes, and PAD by 50-85%+. 
Lifelong Treatment: An ongoing therapy, usually every 2-3 weeks, for life. 
Side Effects: Can include mild numbness, dizziness, or site issues, but risks are generally low compared to benefits. 
Apo B testing
An ApoB (Apolipoprotein B-100) blood test measures the number of particles in your blood that carry "bad" cholesterol (LDL). It is considered a more accurate marker for cardiovascular disease risk than a standard LDL-C test, as it directly counts atherogenic particles. High levels indicate increased risk for plaque buildup and cardiovascular disease, often used to monitor treatment. 
Key Aspects of the ApoB Test:
Purpose: Measures the main protein found in LDL and other fat-carrying particles.
Significance: Because each LDL particle contains one ApoB molecule, this test directly measures the total count of dangerous particles, better identifying risk when LDL cholesterol seems normal
.
When it is used: Recommended for people with diabetes, obesity, high triglycerides, or a family history of heart disease, as well as for monitoring the effectiveness of statin therapy.
Levels: Levels 

 are generally considered to indicate higher risk.
Preparation: An 8-12 hour fast may be required, though requirements vary. 
Common Reasons for High ApoB:
High intake of saturated fats and sugar.
Metabolic conditions like insulin resistance, diabetes, or obesity.
Genetic factors.
Hypothyroidism. 
Differences from Standard Lipid Panel:
While a standard cholesterol panel measures the amount of cholesterol, an ApoB test measures the number of particles, providing a more direct assessment of cardiovascular risk, particularly for people with metabolic syndrome.

Because ApoB measures the total number of atherogenic (plaque-forming) particles in your blood, cardiologists generally agree that lower is better for lifelong heart health. [1, 2, 3]

Average Ranges by Age and Sex
While "average" isn't necessarily "optimal" for heart health, comparing your score to population percentiles can put your numbers into perspective. Typical population ranges (in mg/dL) include: [1]
Age < 40: 50 - 150 mg/dL
Age 40 - 60: 60 - 160 mg/dL
Age > 60: 70 - 170 mg/dL [1, 2, 3, 4]
Note: The general population average often sits around 100 mg/dL, but modern preventive cardiology considers this too high for ideal, long-term cardiovascular health. [1, 2]
Why the Number Matters
ApoB tracks all potentially harmful cholesterol particles (like LDL, VLDL, and Lp(a)) in your bloodstream. In younger adults, ApoB scores can serve as an indicator for cumulative lifetime cardiovascular risk. [1, 2, 3]
