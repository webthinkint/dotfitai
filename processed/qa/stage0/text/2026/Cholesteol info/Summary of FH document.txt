This document reviews familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), its diagnosis, genetic aspects, and management strategies to reduce cardiovascular risk.
Overview of Familial Hypercholesterolemia
Genetic disorder impairing LDL clearance, leading to lifelong elevated LDL cholesterol.
Heterozygous FH affects 1 in 250–350; homozygous FH is rarer, about 1 in 160,000–500,000.
Untreated heterozygous FH causes high risk of coronary events by age 50–60.
Assessment and Diagnosis
Clinical diagnosis relies on LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL and family history of premature ASCVD.
Physical signs include tendon xanthomas, corneal arcus, and xanthelasma.
Evaluation includes family history, physical exam, and lipid testing; genetic testing confirms diagnosis.
Cascade screening of relatives is recommended, especially in families with known FH.
Genetic testing targets LDLR, APOB, PCSK9 genes; it confirms diagnosis and aids cascade screening.
Many with high LDL-C do not have identifiable variants but remain at risk.
Management of Heterozygous FH
Early treatment in childhood offers significant lifetime benefit.
LDL-C goals: less than 70 mg/dL without ASCVD; less than 55 mg/dL with ASCVD.
First-line therapy: high-intensity statins (e.g., rosuvastatin, atorvastatin).
Additional therapies include ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid, or inclisiran if goals are unmet.
Lifestyle modifications: reduce saturated fats, increase activity, manage weight.
Homozygous FH
Usually diagnosed around age 12 with LDL-C 400–1000 mg/dL.
Statins are less effective; LDL apheresis and novel agents like evinacumab and lomitapide are used.
These treatments lower LDL independently of LDL receptor function.
Conclusions
Diagnosis is based on LDL-C levels and family history, not solely genetics.
Early pharmacologic intervention is crucial for reducing lifetime cardiovascular risk.
Combination therapy is often necessary to meet LDL-C targets.
Genetic testing supports cascade screening and treatment decisions, especially in high-risk cases.
