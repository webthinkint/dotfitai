we know that non-essential amino acids can be made from the essential amino acids but 2 questions. 1, can some non-essential amino acids be made from other substrate in the body and 2, when can the ingestion of the total daily amount of non-essential amino acids become a limiting factor in growth and recovery -i.e., could a human live and grow on just the essential amino acids and enough total caloric intake
Yes — many non-essential amino acids (NEAAs) can absolutely be synthesized from substrates other than essential amino acids (EAAs). 
The body commonly makes NEAAs from:
glucose intermediates, 
Krebs/TCA cycle intermediates, 
ammonia/nitrogen transfer, 
fatty acid metabolism indirectly, 
and recycled amino groups from other amino acids. 
Examples:
Pyruvate → alanine 
Oxaloacetate → aspartate 
α-ketoglutarate → glutamate 
Glutamate → glutamine, proline, arginine 
Serine can be made from glycolytic intermediates 
Glycine can be made from serine 
So the body is constantly interconverting carbon skeletons and nitrogen donors to manufacture NEAAs as needed.
That is why they are termed “non-essential” — not because they are unimportant, but because the body usually has the metabolic machinery to synthesize them.
However, there is a critical nuance:
Some “non-essential” amino acids become conditionally essential during:
rapid growth, 
severe stress, 
illness, 
trauma, 
intense training, 
burns, 
aging, 
or recovery states. 
This is especially true for:
glutamine, 
arginine, 
glycine, 
cysteine, 
tyrosine, 
and sometimes proline. 

Yes — there absolutely comes a point where inadequate total NEAA availability can limit growth, recovery, and tissue synthesis, even if EAA intake is sufficient. 
This is one of the most misunderstood concepts in protein metabolism.
EAAs are the trigger and regulatory signal for muscle protein synthesis — especially leucine.
But NEAAs still make up a huge percentage of actual tissue protein structure.
For example:
collagen is heavily dependent on glycine, proline, hydroxyproline, 
glutamine is heavily used by immune cells and gut tissue, 
alanine and glutamate are major nitrogen carriers, 
glycine demand may exceed endogenous production in high-growth states. 
So while EAAs initiate anabolic signaling, the body still requires sufficient total nitrogen and substrate availability to complete actual tissue construction.
A useful analogy:
EAAs are like the “foremen and permits” that authorize building construction. 
NEAAs are still much of the “concrete, steel, drywall, and wiring.” 
You can’t build a skyscraper with only foremen.

Could a human live and grow on only EAAs plus calories?
Theoretically:
A human might survive for a period if supplied all EAAs, calories, vitamins, minerals, fats, and carbohydrates. 
The body could synthesize many NEAAs. 
But in reality:
optimal growth, 
muscle hypertrophy, 
connective tissue repair, 
immune function, 
recovery, 
and long-term health 
would likely become impaired because endogenous synthesis capacity is not unlimited.
Research and clinical nutrition both show:
nitrogen balance, 
recovery, 
wound healing, 
and lean mass retention 
are generally superior when adequate total intact protein or balanced amino acid mixtures are consumed versus EAAs alone.
This is why:
whey protein, 
milk proteins, 
meat, 
eggs, 
and balanced complete proteins 
consistently outperform isolated EAAs alone for long-term tissue support.
Even though EAAs are the indispensable amino acids, the body still benefits enormously from dietary NEAAs because:
it reduces the metabolic burden of synthesizing them, 
it provides nitrogen economy, 
and it supports tissues with unusually high demand for specific NEAAs. 
The modern view is really:
EAAs are indispensable for initiating and permitting growth, but adequate total amino acid availability — including NEAAs — is necessary to sustain maximal growth, repair, recovery, and physiological resilience.
