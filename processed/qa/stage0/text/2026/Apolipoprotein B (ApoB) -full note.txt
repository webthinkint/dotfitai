Apolipoprotein B (ApoB) is the primary structural protein found on the surface of "bad" cholesterol particles, including LDL, VLDL, and triglycerides. Because there is exactly one ApoB protein on every atherogenic (plaque-building) particle, measuring it provides a highly accurate count of artery-clogging particles circulating in your bloodstream. [1, 2, 3]
Why ApoB Matters
While a standard cholesterol test estimates the amount of cholesterol, an ApoB test counts the number of damaging particles. [1]
The "Freeway" Analogy: A standard lipid panel is like counting the number of passengers on the freeway, while an ApoB test counts the actual number of cars.
Hidden Risks: Some people have normal LDL cholesterol levels but a high concentration of small, dense particles. In these cases, ApoB can reveal a high risk of heart disease that a standard test might miss. [1, 2, 3, 4]
When is ApoB Testing Used?
Doctors often order an ApoB blood test (sometimes alongside a standard lipid panel) to further assess cardiovascular risk, especially for patients with: [1, 2, 3]
High triglycerides or metabolic syndrome
Diabetes or insulin resistance
A strong family history of heart disease
Obesity or fatty liver disease [1, 2, 3]
To learn more about how ApoB is utilized to track cardiovascular health, visit the 
American Heart Association or the 
Cleveland Clinic.
Optimal ApoB Targets by Risk Category
Medical organizations like the 

National Lipid Association and the 

European Society of Cardiology recommend the following targets:
Optimal / Low-Risk: <60 - 80 mg/dL
Moderate Risk: <90 mg/dL
High Risk (e.g., existing heart disease or diabetes): <80 mg/dL
Very High Risk (e.g., recurrent cardiovascular events): <65 mg/dL [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
Average Ranges by Age and Sex
While "average" isn't necessarily "optimal" for heart health, comparing your score to population percentiles can put your numbers into perspective. Typical population ranges (in mg/dL) include: [1]
Age < 40: 50 - 150 mg/dL
Age 40 - 60: 60 - 160 mg/dL
Age > 60: 70 - 170 mg/dL [1, 2, 3, 4]
Note: The general population average often sits around 100 mg/dL, but modern preventive cardiology considers this too high for ideal, long-term cardiovascular health. [1, 2]
Why the Number Matters
ApoB tracks all potentially harmful cholesterol particles (like LDL, VLDL, and Lp(a)) in your bloodstream. In younger adults, ApoB scores can serve as an indicator for cumulative lifetime cardiovascular risk. [1, 2, 3]
You can use an 

ApoB Percentile Calculator to see where you fall relative to the rest of the population, but it's important to discuss your specific target with your healthcare provider to account for other factors like blood pressure, genetics, and family history. [1, 2, 3]
