What Happens to Your Hormones When You Don’t Eat Enough Carbs
Key Takeaways:
Very low carbohydrate (carb) intake can shift blood sugar hormones in ways that may make energy feel less predictable.
Carb restriction may alter thyroid, serotonin, and reproductive hormones, affecting mood, metabolism, and menstrual cycle irregularity.
Appetite might decrease as your body adapts to a lower carb intake over time, due to increased leptin sensitivity and stable or decreased ghrelin levels.
When you cut carbs too low for your body’s needs, several hormones shift in an effort to keep you going. Some of these changes can be helpful in the short term, but over time, they may disrupt energy, mood, appetite, and blood sugar control.

1. Low Carbohydrate Intake Can Raise Cortisol Levels

signal safety (feeling full and satisfied after eating) andprovide fuel for energy. When intake decreases, the body often

This is part of a normal stressresponse, but chronically (long-term) elevated cortisol may leave youfeeling wired, fatigued, or hungrier

If carbs are consistently low, your body may respond by producing morecortisol to support blood sugar regulation and energy needs.
These shifts can affect how you feel day to day, so it helps to understandthe signs:
Higher stress levels, even without emotional triggers.
Midday crashes or feeling “tired but wired” at night.

3. Insulin and Glucagon Rise and Fall in NewPatterns
When carb intake drops very low, the glucose-regulating hormones
insulin
and glucagon shift quickly to keep blood sugar steady, which maymake energy levels feel less predictable.
Insulin naturally decreases on lower-carb diets because there is lessglucose to manage
At the same time, glucagon rises to help maintainblood sugar by releasing stored glycogen and making new glucose in theliver.
You might notice it through symptoms such as:
Low energy, especially during exercise
Feeling shaky, light-headed, or irritable between meals

4. Leptin Sensitivity May Improve
When carbohydrate intake drops,
leptin (the satiety hormone) levels
oftendecline, but this does not always lead to increased hunger. As the bodyadapts to a consistent lower-carbohydrate intake over time, the hormonesinvolved in appetite and satiety shift.
Many people on long-term low-carb or
ketogenic diets
experienceimproved leptin sensitivity, meaning the brain becomes better atrecognizing fullness even with lower circulating leptin levels.
At the same time,
ghrelin (the hunger hormone)
is often stable ordecreases, while other satiety hormones like cholecystokinin (CCK) mayrise. This shift makes it easier to feel satisfied with smaller meals.
These appetite-related changes may show up in several ways:
Feeling full sooner during meals
Fewer cravings between meals
A more stable, predictable appetite

Lower leptin levels can also contribute. Leptin helps the brain determine
whether the body has enough stored energy for reproduction, and
research shows that a certain leptin level is important for maintaining

When leptin declines due to low carbohydrate or inadequate fuel intake,

Signs this may be happening include:
Irregular or absent menstrual cycles

Lower energy during the second half (luteal phase) of the cycle.
6. Serotonin May Drop, Affecting Mood
Carbohydrates play a direct role in helping tryptophan (an amino acid)
enter the brain, where it is converted to serotonin (a neurotransmitter
that helps regulate several functions in the brain and body).
Research shows that carbohydrate intake influences serotonin pathways
and satiety signals. When carbs are limited, serotonin production may
fall, which can affect mood, appetite, sleep, and emotional regulation.
Many people notice this in day-to-day emotional patterns:
Feeling moody, irritable, or depressed
Trouble winding down at night
Cravings for carbohydrate-rich comfort foods
