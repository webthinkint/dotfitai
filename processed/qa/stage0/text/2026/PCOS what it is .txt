
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common, often inherited, hormonal disorder affecting 5-13% of women of reproductive age. It is characterized by high levels of androgens (male hormones), irregular periods, and/or small, harmless fluid-filled sacs (follicles) on the ovaries. It is a leading cause of infertility and can lead to long-term issues like Type 2 diabetes if untreated. 
Key Aspects of PCOS
Hormonal Imbalance: The core issue is an overproduction of androgens (such as testosterone) and a lack of progesterone.
Ovulation Issues:
 Due to the imbalance, eggs may not release properly, leading to missed or irregular periods and fertility struggles.
"Polycystic" Misnomer: Despite the name, you do not need to have cysts on your ovaries to have PCOS. The "cysts" are actually follicles containing immature eggs that failed to release.
Insulin Resistance: Many people with PCOS are resistant to insulin, causing the body to produce more, which further increases androgen levels. 
Common Symptoms
Irregular Periods: Infrequent, very long, or missed periods.
Excessive Hair Growth (Hirsutism): Often on the face, chest, or abdomen.
Skin/Hair Changes: Severe acne, dandruff, or male-pattern hair loss (thinning).
Weight Gain: Specifically difficulty losing weight or increased abdominal fat. 
Diagnosis and Management
PCOS is diagnosed using the "Rotterdam criteria," which includes assessing irregular ovulation, high androgen levels, and/or polycystic ovaries via ultrasound. While there is no cure, it is manageable through: 
Lifestyle Changes: Weight loss, a healthy diet, and exercise to manage insulin resistance.
Medication: Birth control pills to regulate periods, metformin for insulin, and anti-androgen medicines for hair/acne. 
Potential Long-Term Health Risks
If left unmanaged, PCOS increases the risk for diabetes, high blood pressure, cholesterol issues, and uterine cancer.
