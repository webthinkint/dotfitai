Yes. Before reconstitution, most peptides are supplied as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder in a sterile vial.
The manufacturing process generally involves dissolving the peptide in solution, sterilizing it, and then freeze-drying it under vacuum to remove the water. What remains is a dry peptide cake or powder attached to the bottom or side of the vial.
A few points:
The "powder" may appear as a white fluffy powder, a compact puck/cake, or a thin film depending on the peptide and manufacturing process. 
The vial typically contains only the peptide and sometimes small amounts of stabilizing excipients (e.g., mannitol), depending on the manufacturer. 
In the dry lyophilized state, peptides are generally much more stable than when dissolved. 
Once bacteriostatic water (BAC water) or sterile water is added, the peptide goes back into solution and begins the clock on its refrigerated-use period. 
After reconstitution, most peptide users store the vial in the refrigerator (typically 36–46°F / 2–8°C), though exact stability varies by peptide and manufacturer. 
For peptides such as BPC-157, TB-500, Tesamorelin, Sermorelin, CJC-1295, and IGF-1 LR3, the vial you receive is normally just a freeze-dried peptide preparation until you add the diluent. The amount of actual peptide present is very small—for example, a "5 mg" vial contains only 5 milligrams of peptide, which is barely visible by itself, which is why the lyophilized cake often looks larger than expected due to the structure created during freeze-drying and any stabilizing ingredients present.

Response 2
Yes. Before reconstitution, most peptides are supplied as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder in a sterile vial.
The manufacturing process typically involves dissolving the peptide in a solution, sterile filtering it, and then removing the water through freeze-drying. What remains is a dry peptide cake or powder that may appear as:
A white or off-white powder 
A small compressed "cake" at the bottom of the vial 
A thin film coating the bottom or side of the vial 
The dry form is used because it is generally much more stable than a liquid solution. Once bacteriostatic water (BAC water) or sterile water is added, the peptide dissolves and becomes a solution suitable for injection according to the prescribed protocol.
A few practical points:
Before reconstitution: many peptides can remain stable for months when stored properly (typically refrigerated, although some are stable at room temperature depending on the manufacturer). 
After reconstitution: stability decreases, which is why most peptide protocols recommend refrigeration and use within a defined period (often 2–8 weeks, depending on the peptide and preparation). 
The amount of BAC water added does not change the total amount of peptide in the vial—it only changes the concentration and therefore how much volume you inject. 
For example:
A vial labeled 10 mg TB-500 contains 10 mg of peptide powder. 
If you add 2 mL of BAC water, the concentration becomes 5 mg/mL. 
If you add 4 mL of BAC water, the concentration becomes 2.5 mg/mL. 
The vial still contains the same total 10 mg of peptide; only the concentration changes.
For peptides you've been discussing recently (Tesamorelin, BPC-157, TB-500, IGF-1 LR3, Sermorelin, etc.), they are typically supplied in this freeze-dried powder form before reconstitution.
