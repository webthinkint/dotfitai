Overall the preponderance of evidence favors supplementation integration in most all circumstances related to aging and anabolic resistance – there is no need to “cherry pick” data – there is far more in favor, which drives the recommendation.
Here are a few of many well-designed, human studies on essential amino acid supplementation in older adults who exercise, with the key details (who, what, dose, training, outcome), and again, most do not factor in protein intakes but that is the point:
Randomized & controlled trials (exercise + EAAs)
Kim et al., 2012 (J Am Geriatrics Soc) — 155 sarcopenic women (≥75 y) randomized to exercise, EAAs (leucine-rich, 3 g twice daily), both, or health education for 3 mo. Exercise + EAA improved knee extension strength (+9.3%) and increased leg muscle mass more than controls; walking speed improved across intervention groups. PubMed
Tokuda et al., 2023 (J Am Nutr Assoc) — Older adults with sarcopenia did supervised resistance training (RT) for 24 wk and were randomized to RT alone vs post-exercise EAAs (3,000 mg) + tea catechins (540 mg). The RT + EAA (+/- catechins) group gained more skeletal muscle mass than RT alone. PubMed
Markofski et al., 2019 (J Gerontol A) — Healthy older men & women (non-frail) in a 2×2 RCT for 24 wk: aerobic exercise (AE) ± daily EAAs. AE + EAAs increased muscle strength and EAA-stimulated MPS; lean mass unchanged. Shows function can improve even when mass doesn’t. PMC
EAA trials in older adults (no formal RT, but relevant to muscle)
4) Dillon et al., 2009 (JCEM) — Healthy older women took EAAs twice daily between meals for 3 mo. Lean body mass increased, and basal MPS and IGF-1 expression rose. PMC
Børsheim et al., 2008 (Clin Nutr) — Glucose-intolerant elderly received EAAs + arginine between meals; lean mass, strength, and physical function improved vs baseline. (Population was older, not specifically training.) PMC
Mechanistic/acute work in older adults (supports dosing & composition around exercise)
6) Katsanos et al., 2006 (Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab) — In the elderly, EAAs must be leucine-rich to optimally stimulate MPS; higher leucine proportion overcame anabolic resistance. PubMed
Volpi et al., 2003 (Am J Clin Nutr) — Demonstrated EAAs are the primary driver of amino-acid–induced muscle protein anabolism in the elderly (vs non-essential AAs). PubMed
Lees et al., 2020 (Nutrients) — Leucine-enriched EAA given after acute RT in older adults increased aminoacidemia and mTORC1 signaling vs placebo, supporting peri-workout EAA use. (Proof-of-concept, signaling outcomes.) PMC
Reviews/position papers that synthesize EAA use in older exercisers
9) Ispoglou et al., 2021 (Proc Nutr Soc) — Review concludes leucine-enriched EAA supplements are a practical way to augment per-meal protein and enhance training responses in older adults; often used at ~7.5 g EAA (≈40% leucine) twice daily. PubMed
10) Cheng et al., 2018 (Br J Nutr) meta-analysis — In mixed older populations with acute/chronic conditions, protein/EAA supplements show variable effects on mass/strength—benefits are more consistent when combined with exercise and in undernourished/frail groups. PubMed

Practical takeaways you can use with older clients who train
Dose per serving: Most effective studies used ~3–15 g EAAs per dose; ensure ~2.5–3+ g leucine to overcome anabolic resistance. PubMed+1
Timing: Give immediately post-exercise (or pre), and/or between meals to “top up” suboptimal meals. PMC+1
Who benefits most: Sarcopenic, frail, or lower-protein older adults show clearer gains (mass/strength). Healthy, well-nourished elders may see functional gains without big mass changes unless resistance loading is sufficient. PubMedPMC
Pair with training: Resistance training remains the main driver of hypertrophy; EAAs amplify the training signal and can improve strength/function. PubMed+1
