Why most chewable and gummy multivitamins may not be a good choice
An estimated 2,500 Americans end up in hospital emergency rooms each year because of trouble swallowing supplements, usually vitamins and minerals. Nearly half are adults 65 and older.
Easier swallowing is one reason why multivitamin gummies, chewables, mints, and liquids are flying off shelves, even though they cost more than tablets. Another is obvious: they taste like candy.
Too bad these easier-to-swallow alternatives don’t measure up to ordinary multivitamins. Many contain far less than recommended levels of vitamins and minerals, and some are missing key nutrients like zinc, magnesium, and chromium.
How do gummy multivitamins stack up?
They were the worst. Most were missing at least five nutrients that a satisfactory multi should contain. So taking more than one at a time won’t help. So, you and your pocketbook will be better off if you can swallow a multivitamin tablet (or choose good complete chewable), or if you can break the tablet up into smaller pieces that are easier to swallow.

In its latest round of supplement policing, natural product manufacturer NOW reported that half the creatine gummy brands it tested failed to meet dosage claim on label. This was not the only red flag raised 
“Gummies are not an ideal form for creatine supplements because water is used to make gummies, so it can be difficult to get the correct dosage of creatine," NOW noted. Creatine gummies: the current flashpoint
This is where the format is clearly struggling.
What we know about creatine stability
Creatine monohydrate is extremely stable in dry powder form—even at elevated temperatures for years—with almost no conversion to creatinine. PMC
In aqueous or acidic environments, creatine degrades substantially over time, especially at low pH and room temperature. Jinfiniti Precision Medicine+1
Gummies are wet, often acidic (citric, malic acids), and heated during production—exactly the conditions that promote creatine breakdown.
Some ingredient suppliers show that with specialized systems (e.g., “beverage-grade” stabilized creatine), you can keep ~70% of creatine intact in gummies after 12 months, which is actually good for a gummy—but still a ~30% loss vs label over a year. Glanbia Nutritionals
Real-world testing of creatine gummies
Independent tests and investigations are ugly:
NOW Foods tested 12 creatine gummy products: 5 failed label claim, with some having “little to none” of the advertised creatine and clear signs of degradation. Nutraceuticals World+1
A 2025 investigation reported several popular Amazon creatine gummies delivering 0–0.005 g creatine per serving instead of the claimed 5 g—basically no effective dose. WIRED
UK testing on another brand found actual amounts far below claim (0.084–0.76 g vs claimed 1.5 g per gummy), meaning users would need dozens of gummies to hit a 5 g daily dose. The Sun
So while a well-engineered creatine gummy is possible in theory, the current marketplace shows frequent underdosing and degradation. For a performance-critical nutrient where dose is everything, that’s a major strike against the format.
Practical consensus in sports nutrition right now:
Creatine monohydrate powder or capsules remain the gold standard. Gummies are, at best, a convenience novelty and, at worst, an unreliable delivery system.
5. Other drawbacks of gummies (beyond potency)
Most medical centers and nutrition experts also call out:
Sugar and dental issues
Gummies commonly contain sugars or sugar alcohols and stick to teeth, raising caries risk, particularly in kids. Cleveland Clinic+2Vitaquest International LLC+2
Caloric load
Multiple gummies per day every day adds sugar and calories that don’t contribute to satiety.
Overconsumption risk
Because they taste like candy, kids (and adults) can easily exceed safe intakes—particularly problematic with iron or fat-soluble vitamins. Cleveland Clinic+1
