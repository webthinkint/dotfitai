Summary
Cyanocobalamin is a safe, effective, and well-researched form of vitamin B12 that has been used successfully in multivitamins for decades. Although it must be converted by the body into the active forms—methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin—healthy individuals generally perform this conversion efficiently, making it an excellent choice for preventing and correcting B12 deficiency. While premium formulations often use the active forms because they require no conversion and align with a "best available ingredients" philosophy, current evidence shows little meaningful clinical advantage over cyanocobalamin for most healthy adults. The small amount of cyanide associated with cyanocobalamin is negligible, naturally detoxified by the body, and not considered a safety concern especially considering the amount is far less than what people ingest naturally from foods such as almonds, flaxseed, lima beans, and cruciferous vegetables. Overall, cyanocobalamin remains a scientifically sound, stable, and reliable source of vitamin B12, while active forms may offer theoretical or niche benefits for certain individuals or premium product positioning. This is why many formulas use both forms.
Is there anything truly wrong with using cyanocobalamin as a form of B12 in a multi?
The short answer is no—there is nothing inherently wrong with using cyanocobalamin in a multivitamin for the vast majority of healthy people. In fact, it has been the form used in many clinical studies and has decades of safety and efficacy data behind it.
That said, there are reasons why some higher-end formulations, particularly those marketed for precision nutrition or healthy aging, choose to use methylcobalamin and/or adenosylcobalamin instead.
Cyanocobalamin: The Pros
Highly stable, making it ideal for multivitamins with long shelf lives. 
Excellent bioavailability at typical supplemental doses. 
Inexpensive, allowing manufacturers to formulate comprehensive products without driving up cost. 
Well studied, with decades of evidence showing it effectively prevents and corrects B12 deficiency in most individuals. 
After absorption, cyanocobalamin is converted by the body into the active coenzyme forms:
Methylcobalamin (used in methylation and nervous system function) 
Adenosylcobalamin (used in mitochondrial energy metabolism) 
Healthy people generally perform this conversion efficiently.
Why Do Some Companies Avoid It?
The main arguments against cyanocobalamin are not that it is ineffective, but that other forms may be preferable for certain populations.
1. Conversion Required
Unlike methylcobalamin, cyanocobalamin is not biologically active until converted by the body.
For healthy adults this is rarely a meaningful limitation.
2. Cyanide Component
The "cyano" portion is often misunderstood.
Each molecule contains one cyanide group, but the amount released is extremely small.
For perspective:
A 500 mcg B12 tablet contains only about 10–15 mcg of cyanide. 
This is far less than what many people ingest naturally from foods such as almonds, flaxseed, lima beans, and cruciferous vegetables. 
The body rapidly detoxifies this tiny amount by converting it primarily to thiocyanate. 
For healthy individuals, this is not considered a safety concern.
3. Certain Medical Conditions
Some clinicians prefer methylcobalamin or hydroxocobalamin for people with:
severe kidney disease 
tobacco amblyopia 
hereditary optic neuropathies 
rare disorders of cyanide metabolism 
These situations are uncommon and generally managed medically.
Is Methylcobalamin Better?
It depends on what "better" means.
Potential advantages:
Already in an active form 
May slightly increase tissue levels in some studies 
Preferred by many functional medicine practitioners 
Often perceived as more "natural" 
However:
Large reviews generally show little clinically meaningful difference between cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin for correcting B12 deficiency in otherwise healthy adults when adequate doses are used. 
Methylcobalamin is less chemically stable, which can be a disadvantage in complex multivitamin formulations. 
For a High-End Multivitamin (like dotFIT)
If you're positioning a premium, practitioner-grade multivitamin, using methylcobalamin (or a combination of methyl- and adenosylcobalamin) aligns well with the overall philosophy of using bioactive nutrient forms. It reinforces the message of providing nutrients in forms the body can use directly, even if the clinical advantage over cyanocobalamin for most users is modest.
Bottom Line
For most healthy people, cyanocobalamin is an effective, safe, and evidence-based form of vitamin B12. It reliably supports B12 status because the body efficiently converts it into the active coenzyme forms. While methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin are attractive options in premium formulations and may offer theoretical advantages for specific individuals, there is no strong evidence that cyanocobalamin is inferior for the general population when consumed at appropriate doses in a multivitamin.
Given your work with trainer education, I would avoid implying that cyanocobalamin is "bad." A more accurate message is that both forms work, but a premium multivitamin may choose active forms to optimize nutrient delivery and align with a "best available ingredient" philosophy rather than because cyanocobalamin is ineffective.
