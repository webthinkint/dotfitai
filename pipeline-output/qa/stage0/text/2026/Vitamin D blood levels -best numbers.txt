For athletes and highly active individuals, many modern sports medicine physicians and performance nutrition specialists aim for a higher serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentration than the minimum needed to prevent deficiency. The goal is to optimize not only bone health but also muscle function, immune resilience, recovery, and potentially reduce injury risk.
Common target ranges
| Serum 25(OH)D | ng/mL | nmol/L | Interpretation |
| Deficient | <20 | <50 | Increased risk of bone and muscle problems |
| Insufficient | 20–30 | 50–75 | Acceptable for many adults but not ideal for athletes |
| Good for general health | 30–50 | 75–125 | Meets most endocrine society recommendations |
| Preferred for many athletes | 40–60 | 100–150 | Frequently targeted by sports physicians and sports dietitians |
| High-normal | 60–80 | 150–200 | Sometimes used in elite athletes under physician supervision |
| Potential concern | >100 | >250 | Generally not recommended without medical indication |
| Possible toxicity | >150 | >375 | Increased risk of hypercalcemia and other adverse effects |
Why 40–60 ng/mL?
This range has become popular among sports medicine clinicians because research suggests that compared with lower concentrations, athletes in this range may experience:
Better muscle strength and power 
Improved muscle protein function 
Reduced incidence of stress fractures 
Better immune function, especially during heavy training 
Fewer upper respiratory infections 
Improved recovery from intense exercise 
Lower risk of muscle strains in athletes who were previously deficient 
The evidence is strongest for correcting deficiency. Benefits above about 40 ng/mL are less consistent, but many experts feel there is little downside to maintaining concentrations between 40 and 60 ng/mL when achieved safely.
Research in athletes
Numerous studies in professional football, soccer, basketball, military personnel, and endurance athletes have shown that athletes with vitamin D levels below 30 ng/mL tend to have:
Higher rates of muscle injury 
More stress fractures 
Longer recovery times 
Reduced muscle function 
When deficient athletes are supplemented to reach approximately 40–50 ng/mL, many studies report improvements in muscle function and reductions in injury rates. Evidence that increasing levels from 50 to 70 ng/mL provides additional performance gains is much weaker.
What many sports physicians actually do
A common approach in elite sports programs is:
Preseason target: 40–60 ng/mL 
In-season maintenance: Keep above 40 ng/mL 
Recheck every 3–6 months, particularly in winter or in indoor athletes 
This is especially common in:
NFL 
NBA 
NHL 
Professional soccer 
Olympic training centers 
Practical supplementation
Although individual needs vary greatly:
1,000 IU/day typically raises serum 25(OH)D by roughly 5–10 ng/mL over several months. 
Many athletes require 2,000–4,000 IU/day to maintain levels between 40 and 60 ng/mL, especially those with darker skin, limited sun exposure, higher body fat, or who train indoors. 
Some deficient athletes require short-term higher doses under physician supervision before transitioning to a maintenance dose. 
For your work with athletes
Given your focus on body composition, performance, and recovery, I would summarize the current practical consensus as:
Maintain serum 25(OH)D between approximately 40 and 60 ng/mL (100–150 nmol/L). This range is commonly targeted by sports medicine physicians because it appears to provide a favorable balance between musculoskeletal health, immune function, and recovery while avoiding unnecessarily high concentrations. There is little evidence that routinely pushing levels above 60–70 ng/mL produces additional athletic benefits in healthy individuals.
This recommendation is consistent with the practices of many sports medicine clinicians and is more aggressive than the 30 ng/mL minimum often cited for the general population, while remaining well below concentrations associated with toxicity.
