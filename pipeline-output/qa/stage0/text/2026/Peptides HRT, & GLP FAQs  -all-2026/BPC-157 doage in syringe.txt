To answer this accurately, we need one missing piece of information:
How much liquid (mL) was used to reconstitute the BPC-157 vial, and how many total micrograms are in the vial (e.g., 5 mg)?
The number on an insulin syringe (10–100) does not represent micrograms. It represents volume (mL or “units”), so the microgram dose depends entirely on the final concentration after reconstitution.
That said, below is a clear, practical guide using the most common real-world setups, so you can see exactly how this works.

Key syringe facts (important)
A U-100 insulin syringe:
100 units = 1.0 mL
10 units = 0.10 mL
20 units = 0.20 mL
etc.

Syringe units =

How much volume is in a typical insulin syringe?
A standard U-100 insulin syringe holds:
1.0 mL total volume

What do the numbers 10–100 mean?
The numbers do NOT represent milligrams or micrograms.
They represent insulin units, which are simply a way of marking volume.
For a U-100 syringe:
100 units = 1.0 mL
10 units = 0.10 mL
1 unit = 0.01 mL

Why this matters (especially for peptides)
Insulin syringe numbers = volume only
The actual dose (mcg or mg) depends on:
How much powder was in the vial
How much liquid (mL) it was mixed with
So:
The syringe never tells you the dose — only how much liquid you’re drawing.
