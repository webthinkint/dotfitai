Important safety framing (for you + any trainers reading this):
With the exception of tesamorelin (for a very specific HIV-related indication) and elamipretide in narrow rare-disease contexts, these peptides are not FDA-approved for general use, quality control is highly variable, human data are sparse, and most “protocols” are based on uncontrolled experience. Nothing below is medical advice or a recommendation to use—only a map of what’s out there so trainers can have informed conversations and know when to refer out.
A. Science-Heavy Expanded Guide
1. TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment)
What it is / status
Synthetic fragment of the endogenous peptide thymosin beta-4, involved in tissue repair and cell migration. PMC
Used experimentally for soft-tissue healing; not FDA-approved for any indication.
Mechanisms (proposed)
Binds to G-actin, promoting actin polymerization, cell migration, and cytoskeletal remodeling in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myocytes. PMC
Upregulates angiogenesis (VEGF, endothelial cell migration), decreases inflammatory cytokines, and may reduce fibrosis. PMC+1
Evidence snapshot
Animal data: improved healing in cardiac injury, corneal wounds, dermal wounds, and some musculoskeletal models.   PMC
Human data remain limited and mostly small/uncontrolled.
Dosing in literature / practice (informational only)
Formal dose-finding in humans is sparse; clinical monographs and compounding protocols often describe systemic doses in the low mg per week range, divided into several subcutaneous injections. A4M
True evidence-based “optimal” dosing is unknown.
Risks / concerns
Short-term: injection-site reactions, edema, fatigue, headache; overall tolerability in small series appears reasonable.Swolverine+1
Theoretical risks: pro-angiogenic and pro-migration effects raise concern about tumor promotion or unwanted angiogenesis in susceptible individuals (cancer history, proliferative retinopathies, etc.), though this has not been definitively shown in humans.PMC
Long-term human safety is essentially unknown.
2. BPC-157 (Body Protection Compound-157)
What it is / status
15-aa peptide derived from a gastric protein thought to protect and repair gut mucosa. PMC
No FDA approval; banned by WADA and many sport bodies. PMC+1
Mechanisms (proposed)
Potent pro-angiogenic actions (VEGF signaling), enhances fibroblast migration, collagen deposition, and tendon/ligament repair in preclinical models .Physiology Journals+1
Modulates NO signaling, exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, and supports gut barrier integrity. PMC+2PMC+2
Evidence snapshot
Robust rodent data: improved healing of tendons, ligaments, muscles, bone, and GI tract; some neuroprotective signals. Physiology Journals+1
Human evidence is minimal and largely anecdotal; a recent narrative review emphasizes high promise but very low-quality human data. PMC
Dosing in literature / practice (informational only)
Most data are in animals (e.g., microgram/kg injections).Physiology Journals
Compounded “clinic” protocols commonly cite ~250–500 mcg once or twice daily, oral or SC, but this is not evidence-based and often extrapolated from preclinical work.
Risks / concerns
The new 2025 review highlights that while preclinical safety seems acceptable, the lack of regulated manufacturing, dosing standards, or long-term human follow-up is a major concern.PMC+1
Potential issues: injection-site reactions, unknown effects on aberrant angiogenesis (e.g., tumors, diabetic retinopathy), possible drug–drug interactions through endothelial/NO pathways.

3. MOTS-c
What it is / status
16-aa mitochondria-derived peptide encoded by mtDNA, secreted in response to metabolic stress.Cell
Investigational only; no current broad FDA indication.
Mechanisms
Activates AMPK, increases glucose uptake, and shifts muscle toward fatty acid oxidation and improved insulin sensitivity.PMC+2Cell+2
Modulates expression of nuclear genes related to metabolism and proteostasis (exercise-like transcriptional signature).BioMed Central+1
Evidence snapshot
Animal studies: improved exercise capacity, metabolic flexibility, and healthspan with intermittent dosing in aged mice.Nature+1
Early human data: small studies/observations linking MOTS-c to exercise response and metabolic health; interventional human trials remain very limited.
Dosing in literature / practice (informational only)
Mouse work commonly uses intermittent injections several times per week; dose scaled in mg/kg.Nature+1
Compounding-clinic “protocols” often describe 5–15 mg SC, 2–3x/week, extrapolated from animal scaling—this is experimental and not validated.
Risks / concerns
Human safety data are sparse; theoretical concerns involve over-activation of AMPK, unintended weight loss, hypoglycemia in susceptible patients, or interactions with other metabolic drugs.
Unknown effects in cancer, given metabolic reprogramming roles.

4. SS-31 / Elamipretide
What it is / status
Synthetic mitochondria-targeted tetrapeptide that binds cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane.ScienceDirect+1
Investigated in cardiomyopathy, Barth syndrome, and other mitochondrial disorders; regulatory status is evolving and indication-specific (recent FDA decisions have been mixed).The Hub+1
Mechanisms
Binds cardiolipin, stabilizing inner mitochondrial membrane structure and respiratory chain complexes, reducing ROS production and improving ATP generation.ScienceDirect+1
Has antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects in high-stress tissues like heart and skeletal muscle.
Evidence snapshot
In Barth syndrome, longer-term elamipretide improved functional capacity and cardiac stroke volume in a phase 2/3 trial, though regulatory endpoints have been contentious.PMC+2Nature+2
Multiple mitochondrial disease and heart failure trials show mixed but encouraging signals on exercise tolerance and mitochondrial biomarkers.ScienceDirect
Dosing (from trials, not a protocol)
Trials often use daily SC or IV dosing at fixed mg amounts (e.g., ~40 mg/day SC in some human studies), but regimens are disease- and protocol-specific.PMC+1
Risks / concerns
Reported adverse effects: injection-site reactions, transient headache, nausea, and some GI symptoms; overall tolerability moderate in trials.PMC+1
As a drug under active regulatory scrutiny, use belongs inside clinical trials / specialist care, not fitness settings.

5. AOD-9604 (hGH Fragment 176–191)
What it is / status
Synthetic fragment of the C-terminus of human GH, designed to retain lipolytic but not growth-promoting properties.PMC+1
Failed to demonstrate consistent weight-loss efficacy in human obesity trials; development as an anti-obesity drug was discontinued, though some clinics still market it off-label.PMC+2paragonsportsmedicine.com+2
Mechanisms
Activates lipolysis and inhibits lipogenesis in adipose tissue, likely via cAMP-mediated signaling, without robust GH-like effects on IGF-1 or glucose.PMC+1
Evidence snapshot
Animal studies: meaningful reductions in body fat in obese mice.OUP Academic
Human trials: relatively benign safety profile across several hundred participants, but inconsistent or modest efficacy on fat loss → program was largely abandoned.PMC+2jofem.org+2
Dosing (research)
Obesity trials used oral or injectable daily dosing at various mg ranges; exact regimen depends on protocol.PMC+1
Commercial peptide clinics often use daily SC injections, but there is no evidence-based standard.
Risks / concerns
Trials suggest good short-term safety—GI symptoms, headache, mild joint issues most common.jofem.org+1
Biggest concern is overpromising fat-loss efficacy and using it as a crutch instead of foundational lifestyle changes.

6. Epitalon / Epithalamin
What it is / status
Synthetic version of a tetrapeptide originally isolated from the pineal gland; studied as a “geroprotective” peptide.PMC+1
Not FDA-approved; research is mostly from Eastern European/Russian groups plus newer mechanistic work.
Mechanisms
Induces telomerase expression and telomere elongation in human somatic cells in vitro.PMC+1
Appears to modulate clock genes, melatonin secretion, antioxidant defenses, and immune balance, potentially affecting circadian rhythm, redox homeostasis, and inflammation.PMC+2Healthspan+2
Evidence snapshot
Some long-term clinical observations suggest reduced mortality and improved markers of aging in elderly cohorts receiving pineal peptides, but methodology is often limited and not widely replicated.Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation+1
High-quality, modern placebo-controlled human trials are still lacking.
Dosing (reported in research)
Protocols from Russian trials often used short courses (10–20 days) of low-mg daily injections, repeated annually or semi-annually, but details vary widely.Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation+1
Risks / concerns
Available data suggest good tolerability in older adults over years, but much of this is from a single research ecosystem.Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation
Theoretical risk: telomerase activation could promote survival of pre-malignant cells; cancer risk needs much better characterization.

7. Kisspeptin (Kisspeptin-10, -54)
What it is / status
Endogenous peptide family critical for activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis.PMC+1
Studied as a diagnostic and therapeutic tool in reproductive medicine (e.g., infertility, IVF triggers).Frontiers+1
Mechanisms
Binds KISS1R (GPR54) on GnRH neurons → stimulates GnRH release, which increases pituitary LH/FSH, leading to increased gonadal steroid production.PMC+1
Integrates steroid feedback and environmental cues (e.g., energy availability) into reproductive hormone output.
Evidence snapshot
Human work: bolus IV kisspeptin robustly increases LH/FSH in both men and women; kisspeptin is being trialed as a safer oocyte maturation trigger in IVF.PMC+1
Dosing (research)
IVF and endocrine research uses carefully titrated IV or SC boluses/infusions under specialist supervision; mg ranges are protocol-specific and not appropriate for “recreational” copying.Frontiers+1
Risks / concerns
Acute flushing, nausea, headache; risk of ovarian hyperstimulation if misused in IVF contexts.Frontiers
Not appropriate for gym-level optimization; belongs entirely in reproductive endocrinology settings.

8. Tesamorelin
What it is / status
Synthetic GHRH analog, FDA-approved as EGRIFTA® / EGRIFTA SV / EGRIFTA WR for HIV-associated lipodystrophy with excess visceral fat. FDA Access Data+1
Mechanisms
Binds pituitary GHRH receptors, increasing pulsatile GH secretion and thereby raising IGF-1 and promoting lipolysis, especially in visceral depots. FDA Access Data
Evidence snapshot
Multiple RCTs show reductions in visceral adipose tissue and modest metabolic effects in HIV patients; GH/IGF-1 axis clearly stimulated. FDA Access Data+1
Label dosing (for its approved indication, not a gym protocol)
Labeled regimen is 1.4 mg SC once daily in the abdomen for HIV-associated lipodystrophy, with detailed monitoring recommendations. FDA Access Data+1
Risks / concerns
Hypersensitivity reactions (rash, urticaria, even anaphylaxis), injection-site reactions, arthralgia, edema, carpal tunnel–like symptoms, increased IGF-1, and potential glucose intolerance / diabetes risk.FDA Access Data+2Mayo Clinic+2
Contraindicated in active malignancy and used cautiously in anyone with a cancer history because of GH/IGF-1 axis activation.

9. CJC-1295 (with/without DAC)
What it is / status
Synthetic GHRH analog; “with DAC” version is conjugated to increase half-life to days.
Not FDA-approved; used off-label in anti-aging / performance circles.
Mechanisms
Binds GHRH receptors → increases amplitude and duration of endogenous GH pulses, especially in response to natural stimuli (sleep, exercise).
With DAC, maintains elevated baseline GHRH signaling over several days, yielding a sustained rise in GH and IGF-1.
Evidence snapshot
Early clinical work showed dose-dependent increases in GH and IGF-1 over weeks; long-term outcome data on body composition, CV risk, or cancer are lacking.
Dosing in literature / practice (informational)
Trials used single or weekly injections at fixed mcg/kg doses; “clinic” protocols often mention ~1–2 mg once or twice weekly with DAC, or smaller, more frequent dosing without DAC—none of this is outcome-validated.
Risks / concerns
Similar to other GH axis stimulators: edema, arthralgia, paresthesias, carpal tunnel–type symptoms, potential worsening of sleep apnea, and theoretical tumor-promotion risk.
Additive risks when stacked with other GHRPs or GH itself.


Trainer-Friendly Version (Talking Points & Guardrails)
You can treat this as your coach-facing crib sheet when clients ask about peptides.
Quick Legend
Status: ✅ FDA-approved for narrow indication | 🔬 Experimental | 🚫 Not approved / banned in sport
Trainer rule of thumb: You can explain mechanisms & risks, but you do not recommend, source, or help dose these. Always refer to a qualified medical provider.
1. TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4 fragment) – 🔬 Experimental
What clients think it does: “Heals injuries faster, helps joints and tendons.”
Plain-English mechanism: Signals cells involved in repair to move in, build new blood vessels, and remodel tissue.
Key risks to mention: Long-term safety unknown; in theory anything that boosts blood vessel growth and cell migration could be a problem in people with cancer or eye disease.
Trainer line: “Interesting early data in animals, but human safety’s not nailed down—this is something to discuss with a sports med or regenerative-medicine MD, not something we handle on the gym floor.”
2. BPC-157 – 🚫 Not approved, banned in sport
What clients think it does: “Fixes everything—gut, tendon, ligament, shoulder, you name it.”
Plain-English mechanism: Acts like a repair signal that can boost blood flow and collagen formation in injured tissues and may protect the gut lining.
Key risks: Human trials are almost non-existent; quality is all over the place; potential concern for abnormal blood vessel growth and unknown long-term effects. It is prohibited by many sports organizations.PMC+1
Trainer line: “Because it’s banned in sport and not well-studied in humans, I’d want your physician fully in the loop before you even consider it.”
3. MOTS-c – 🔬 Experimental “exercise-mimetic” signal
What clients think it does: “Gives you better energy, fat burning, and endurance.”
Plain-English mechanism: A natural mitochondrial signal that makes muscles handle fuel more like a trained athlete—better glucose handling and more fat use.BioMed Central+1
Key risks: Very new; we don’t know long-term effects on blood sugar, weight, or cancer risk.
Trainer line: “Lifestyle, training, and nutrition already drive the same AMPK pathways that MOTS-c targets—and those are proven and safe.”
4. SS-31 / Elamipretide – 🔬 Rare-disease drug candidate
What clients think it does: “Repairs mitochondria and boosts energy/heart function.”
Plain-English mechanism: Sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane and helps your powerplants make energy with less oxidative damage.ScienceDirect+1
Key risks: Still being sorted out in clinical trials; absolutely not a general performance peptide.
Trainer line: “Right now this is a specialist drug for rare mitochondrial issues, not a mainstream recovery tool.”
5. AOD-9604 – 🔬 Safe-ish, but weak evidence for fat loss
What clients think it does: “GH-like fat burner without side effects.”
Plain-English mechanism: A small piece of GH that was engineered to tilt fat cells toward breaking down fat instead of storing it, without much impact on growth.PMC+1
Key risks: Human trials show it’s well-tolerated but not very impressive for weight loss; the big risk is people using it instead of fixing food, steps, and training.PMC+1
Trainer line: “Even in clinical trials AOD didn’t move the needle much—your calories, protein, steps, and sleep still do 99% of the work.”
6. Epitalon – 🔬 Longevity / anti-aging peptide
What clients think it does: “Reverses aging, lengthens telomeres, fixes sleep.”
Plain-English mechanism: May turn on telomerase, help keep telomeres from shortening, and tune circadian and antioxidant systems.PMC+1
Key risks: Aging and cancer are tightly linked—boosting telomerase in the wrong person is a theoretical issue; long-term, high-quality studies are thin.Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation
Trainer line: “Even if the longevity angle pans out, this is something that belongs in a medical anti-aging clinic, not a casual stack.”
7. Kisspeptin – ✅/🔬 Medical reproductive tool, not a “test booster”
What clients think it does: “Boosts testosterone, helps fertility, fixes libido.”
Plain-English mechanism: It’s basically the on-switch for the brain’s reproductive hormone cascade (GnRH → LH/FSH → testosterone/estrogen).PMC+1
Key risks: Can strongly affect fertility hormones; in IVF settings, misuse can cause ovarian hyperstimulation and other serious issues.Frontiers
Trainer line: “Any hormone-level work like this is strictly for an endocrinologist or fertility specialist.”
8. Tesamorelin – ✅ FDA-approved (very specific use)
What clients think it does: “Lean-out peptide that burns belly fat by boosting GH.”
Plain-English mechanism: Mimics the brain’s GHRH signal, which makes the pituitary release more GH in pulses, raising IGF-1 and helping reduce visceral fat.FDA Access Data
Key risks: Can raise blood sugar, IGF-1, and cause edema, joint pain, numbness, and allergic reactions; contraindicated in active cancer.FDA Access Data+1
Trainer line: “It’s a real, prescription-only drug for a specific HIV-related condition—if someone’s using it outside that, they absolutely need a physician supervising.”
9. CJC-1295 (with or without DAC) – 🔬 GH-axis stimulator
What clients think it does: “Long-acting GH booster for recovery, lean mass, and sleep.”
Plain-English mechanism: Acts like a long-lasting GHRH, making the body release more of its own GH and IGF-1 over time.
Key risks: Same issues you’d worry about with elevated GH/IGF-1: edema, carpal-tunnel-type symptoms, possible blood sugar and cancer-promotion concerns, plus no long-term outcome data.
Trainer line: “If someone’s trying to get GH-like effects, they also inherit the GH-like risks—this is doctor territory, not coaching territory.”
How Trainers Can Use This in Practice
1. Normalize curiosity, but anchor in basics.
“It makes sense you’re hearing a lot about peptides; they’re a hot topic. The things we know extend healthspan and performance are still: training, protein, sleep, weight control, and smart supplementation we can fully vet.”
2. Draw the “scope of practice” line clearly.
“I can explain what these compounds try to do and what the risks are, but I don’t prescribe, source, or advise doses. If you’re serious about them, we pull a qualified physician into the conversation.”
3. Use peptides as a door to better fundamentals.
If a client wants BPC-157 for tendon pain → talk load management, movement quality, protein, collagen + vitamin C, and sleep, and suggest an ortho/sports-med consult
