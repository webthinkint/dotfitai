General scientific consensus
Buccal delivery of peptides is scientifically plausible, but ordinary dissolving strips should not be assumed to deliver a therapeutically meaningful or reliable dose. The buccal cavity avoids stomach acid, intestinal enzymes, and some first-pass liver metabolism. However, the cheek lining remains an effective biological barrier, particularly against molecules that are relatively large, water-soluble, and electrically charged—which describes most therapeutic peptides. Reviews consistently identify low permeability, enzymatic degradation in saliva and mucosal tissue, short contact time, swallowing of the dose, and substantial person-to-person variability as major obstacles. 
Therefore, the consensus is closer to:
Buccal peptide delivery is a promising drug-delivery research platform, but it is not a generally validated substitute for injection unless the specific peptide and finished formulation have demonstrated human pharmacokinetics and clinical efficacy.
A strip can dissolve in the mouth without the peptide actually crossing the mucosa. Much of the dissolved material may simply be swallowed and then degraded.
What is needed for a strip to work?
Successful formulations generally require more than putting peptide powder into a dissolvable film. Research systems often combine several technologies:
A mucoadhesive polymer that keeps the drug against the cheek. 
A permeation enhancer, such as a surfactant, bile salt, fatty-acid derivative, cyclodextrin, chitosan, or cell-penetrating peptide. 
Protection from enzymatic degradation. 
Precise control of peptide stability, concentration, film dissolution, pH and contact time. 
Sometimes nanoparticles, liposomes, chemical peptide modification or directional films that prevent drug loss into saliva. 
Even with these measures, exposure is often low or variable.
Are there supporting studies?
Yes, but most support the concept rather than proving that currently marketed wellness strips work.
Insulin
Insulin is the most studied example. A small proof-of-concept human study of an oral/buccal insulin spray reported reductions in post-meal glucose, demonstrating that some biologically active insulin could be delivered through the oral cavity. However, results with buccal insulin systems have generally shown variable absorption, and no conventional insulin cheek strip has become an established replacement for injected insulin. 
More recent insulin research frequently involves sophisticated carriers or cell-penetrating peptides rather than a simple dissolving strip. For example, a 2020 study found improved buccal insulin transport using a specialized cell-penetrating-peptide system, but this was preclinical formulation research—not proof that an ordinary retail strip produces equivalent systemic exposure in humans. 
Calcitonin
Salmon calcitonin has also been investigated because it is a potent peptide active at comparatively small doses. Studies using penetratin-conjugated liposomes have demonstrated improved buccal permeation, but the evidence has largely been in vitro or ex vivo, rather than large clinical outcome trials. 
Other peptides
Laboratory and animal research has examined buccal delivery of insulin, calcitonin, TRH and other peptides, often showing that absorption can be increased using penetration enhancers. These findings establish biological feasibility, but they cannot automatically be extrapolated to BPC-157, CJC-1295, ipamorelin, TB-500, sermorelin or similar wellness peptides. 
Regarding BPC-157, CJC-1295, ipamorelin and similar strips
I could not find credible published human pharmacokinetic trials demonstrating that commercially marketed buccal strips containing these peptides produce:
Predictable blood concentrations, 
A stated percentage of absolute bioavailability, 
Comparable exposure to injection, or 
Clinically meaningful outcomes. 
Claims such as “90% or 92% bioavailability” should be treated cautiously unless the seller can provide a peer-reviewed human crossover study comparing the finished strip with an intravenous or subcutaneous reference product. A company’s dissolution test, ingredient assay, testimonial or general research on buccal technology does not establish systemic bioavailability for its product.
This concern is separate from whether the peptide itself works. For several commonly marketed compounds, including BPC-157, CJC-1295 and ipamorelin, the FDA has cited limited safety information, peptide-related impurity concerns and potential immunogenicity issues. 
Practical conclusion
The delivery route is credible in principle, but most peptide strips currently being promoted are ahead of the supporting clinical science. A properly engineered buccal pharmaceutical may deliver a particular peptide, especially a small and highly potent one. But efficacy is molecule- and formulation-specific; it cannot be inferred merely because a strip is placed against the cheek.
For evaluating a particular product, I would ask the manufacturer for:
Human pharmacokinetic data on the finished strip, not just the raw peptide. 
Absolute or relative bioavailability with a clearly identified comparator. 
Blood concentration curves—Cmax, Tmax and AUC. 
Independent potency, purity and stability testing. 
Evidence that the dose remains intact through its expiration date. 
Published human clinical outcomes at the strip’s actual dose. 
Without those data, the most scientifically accurate description is experimental and unproven, rather than demonstrably ineffective—but it should not be presented as comparable to injection.
