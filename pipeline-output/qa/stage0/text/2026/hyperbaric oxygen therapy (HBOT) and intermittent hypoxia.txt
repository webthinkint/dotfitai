Yes. There is science behind both hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) and intermittent hypoxia, but they should be viewed quite differently if the goal is exercise recovery.
1. HBOT — promising, but not yet a slam dunk for routine recovery
HBOT exposes someone to nearly 100% oxygen at elevated atmospheric pressure, commonly around 1.5–2.5 ATA. This dramatically increases dissolved oxygen in plasma and oxygen delivery to tissues.
The most recent evidence is more favorable than some of the older reviews. A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of 10 randomized studies involving 299 subjects concluded that HBOT significantly accelerated recovery from exercise-induced muscle injury, although it did not significantly improve muscle soreness overall. Benefits were seen with pressures both above and below 2.0 ATA and with 60- and 100-minute sessions. 
Another 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis found that HBOT significantly reduced post-exercise blood lactate, although effects on short-term power and force production were limited. 
That is noteworthy because an earlier 2021 meta-analysis had concluded that HBOT before exercise did not significantly improve performance and post-exercise HBOT did not significantly improve recovery. So the evidence is evolving, but still inconsistent.
Mechanistically, HBOT is plausible for recovery because hyperoxia can influence tissue oxygenation, edema, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, angiogenesis and tissue repair. But those biological effects don't necessarily translate into faster recovery from an ordinary hard workout.
My rating for routine exercise recovery: 🟡 Promising, but not established.
I would view HBOT more favorably for tissue injury/rehabilitation situations than as something every healthy gym member needs after training.

2. Intermittent hypoxia — stronger science, but primarily for adaptation/performance, not acute recovery
This one is fascinating because we're essentially doing the opposite:
HBOT → temporarily increase oxygen availability
Intermittent hypoxia → temporarily decrease oxygen availability
Intermittent hypoxia exposes someone to repeated periods of reduced oxygen—often equivalent to being at altitude—followed by periods of normal oxygen.
The resulting physiological stress can stimulate adaptations involving HIF-1α signaling, mitochondrial function, vascular adaptations, oxygen utilization and metabolic efficiency.
There is now a substantial body of evidence around hypoxic training. A recent umbrella review included 22 systematic reviews covering 487 studies and 5,333 participants. Overall, intermittent-hypoxia approaches improved aerobic and anaerobic performance and, in some circumstances, strength. 
And an especially interesting 2026 network meta-analysis of 29 randomized trials involving 603 trained athletes found:
Intermittent hypoxic training significantly improved aerobic capacity 
Repeated-sprint training in hypoxia improved both aerobic and anaerobic capacity 
The effects depended considerably on the protocol and simulated altitude. 
However, here's the important distinction for your question:
Intermittent hypoxia has much better evidence as a training/adaptation stimulus than as a post-workout recovery treatment.
So I wouldn't currently sell a 20–30 minute hypoxia session after someone's workout as a scientifically established way to "recover faster."

An interesting third option: Hypoxia + Hyperoxia
There are systems that alternate:
Hypoxia → Hyperoxia → Hypoxia → Hyperoxia
sometimes called intermittent hypoxia-hyperoxia (IHH).
A systematic review found improvements in exercise tolerance and peak oxygen uptake, among other outcomes, although there were only eight qualifying studies and the populations varied substantially. 
The concept is biologically interesting because you are creating a controlled oxygen stress followed by oxygen-rich recovery periods—essentially using oxygen availability as a physiological conditioning stimulus.
But again, I'd classify this as emerging, rather than proven exercise recovery technology.
If we're comparing the recovery technologies we've been discussing
| Modality | Acute muscle recovery | Performance/adaptation | Injury/rehab | Evidence rating |
| Red/NIR PBM | ★★★★ | ★★★ | ★★★ | Good/promising |
| HBOT | ★★★ | ★★ | ★★★★ | Promising/mixed |
| Intermittent hypoxia | ★★ | ★★★★ | ★★ | Good for adaptation |
| Hypoxia/Hyperoxia | ★★ | ★★★ | ★★ | Emerging |
One caveat: those stars are my practical interpretation of the literature, not a validated scientific grading system.
For a gym Recovery Zone, I'd therefore put Red/NIR ahead of HBOT specifically for routine post-training recovery. HBOT becomes more compelling when the target moves toward tissue healing/rehabilitation, while intermittent hypoxia belongs more naturally on the conditioning/performance/longevity-adaptation side of the facility than in the pure recovery category. 
There is also an interesting question here that I think is worth investigating: Red/NIR + compression/recovery chair + oxygen/hypoxia modalities and whether there is evidence for combining them or sequencing them. That could help determine which technologies actually deserve space in a gym recovery center versus which ones mainly have good marketing.
