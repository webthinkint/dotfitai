2. Creatine and perimenopause: promising early findings — Oct. 24. A randomized study of 51 women found better sleep consistency and fewer reported temperature-sensitivity days with creatine plus resistance training versus placebo plus training. Stroop cognitive-test performance did not differ between groups. This was a short, six-week exploratory analysis with wearable/self-reported outcomes and disclosed industry relationships. Trainer takeaway: worth following, but don’t promise a brain-fog cure or menopause treatment. Original study; Original study

Creatine Study Summary And Guidance
This attached 12-week randomized, double-blind trial in 64 healthy adults aged 45–65 found that 10 g/day creatine monohydrate increased lean mass with or without training, and improved strength more when combined with exercise and dieting, while cognitive and metabolic signals were encouraging but explicitly exploratory . It also reported good tolerability, with small creatinine rises that remained within normal ranges and should not be read as proven kidney harm in healthy adults.
For most healthy adults who want to stay active, this study strengthens prior evidence that creatine monohydrate is most useful as a muscle-preserving and strength-supporting supplement, especially alongside resistance training and during weight loss, because the exercise-plus-creatine group gained lean tissue and lost a greater percentage of body fat than exercise plus placebo, while even non-exercisers gained lean mass and some strength. That fits older and broader data showing creatine reliably raises muscle creatine stores by about 20%–40%, generally improves strength and training adaptations, and meta-analytic and review data cited in the paper support benefits for muscle mass in both trained and untrained adults, with more modest and less consistent evidence for fat loss and cognition . The cognitive findings here are interesting but not definitive, because some memory and reaction-time measures improved, many others did not, and the authors prespecified individual cognitive outcomes as exploratory without multiplicity adjustment . Going forward, the main implication is that creatine should be studied less as a bodybuilding aid and more as a healthy aging adjunct for preserving lean mass during aging, exercise, dieting, and possibly weight-loss drug use, with future trials needing better kidney markers such as cystatin C or measured GFR, higher-protein diets, and adequately powered cognitive endpoints. For healthy adults desiring to remain active, the evidence supports that research commonly evaluates 3–5 g/day as a standard long-term dose and this trial used 10 g/day to probe possible cognitive effects; any personal choice should be discussed with a licensed clinician, especially for people with kidney disease, unusual lab results, or relevant medications, because this is evidence synthesis rather than individualized medical advice

Whole-grain structure matters — new study, Oct. 26. In a randomized trial of 160 adults with type 2 diabetes, 12 weeks of less-processed whole grains lowered HbA1c slightly more than milled whole grains, by about 0.2 percentage points, without a weight difference. The effect was modest and the trial short. Trainer takeaway: choosing more intact grains can be a useful dietary refinement. Original study
Muscle remains responsive after critical illness — Oct. 24. Twenty ICU survivors and 20 comparison participants reached similar muscle-protein-synthesis rates after resistance exercise plus protein, despite the survivors’ weakness. This acute experiment supports the rationale for supervised exercise-and-nutrition rehabilitation; it did not test long-term muscle or strength recovery. Original study
DHA and cognition: what the fish-oil headline actually supports. An Oct. 28 headline revisits a June 18 trial, rather than a new study. Among 365 adults aged 55–80 with dementia risk factors, 2 g/day DHA increased a cerebrospinal-fluid DHA measure but showed no cognitive or brain-volume advantage over two years. Cognition was exploratory, and dropout was 38%, largely because of COVID. Practitioner takeaway: this trial does not support promising dementia prevention from DHA; it does not settle other uses of omega-3s. Original trial
Internet trend: “fibermaxxing” — useful goal, product claims need checking. Oct. 28 coverage includes fiber-rich recipes and fiber-branded products. Increasing an inadequate fiber intake is sensible; a trendy label alone says little about a product’s value. Trainer takeaway: consider fiber type, dose and the overall diet, and increase intake gradually rather than chasing the highest number. Dated trend assessment

Neal, here’s your October 30 science and supplement roundup.
Ketone supplements for recovery: evidence remains unconvincing — Oct. 29. A systematic review of 16 studies found no clear improvements in soreness, glycogen replenishment or creatine kinase, and no consistent physical-recovery benefit. Evidence mostly involved active men. Trainer takeaway: this review doesn’t establish a dependable recovery benefit to justify routine use. Original review
Ginseng + HMB formula: promising, population-specific findings — Oct. 13 study, receiving Oct. 29 coverage. In a six-month randomized trial of 136 adults aged 65+ with cognitive impairment and sarcopenia, the combined formula improved grip strength by about 1.44 kg versus matched nutrition; cognitive scores also improved. Muscle-mass findings were less robust. A single center, retrospective registration and supplier-affiliated authors warrant caution. Practitioner takeaway: worth following, but it cannot isolate HMB’s or ginseng’s contribution or establish the same benefit in healthy clients. Original study
Handgrip exercise and blood pressure — Oct. 25. A four-week randomized trial in 67 medicated adults found lower daytime blood pressure after supervised handgrip training versus control. There was no significant benefit for 24-hour readings. Crucially, baseline ambulatory pressure wasn’t measured, limiting conclusions about changes. Trainer takeaway: preliminary support for an adjunct to care, with clinician-guided programming for clients with hypertension. Original study
Trend watch: “cozy cardio” — potentially useful for participation. Oct. 26 coverage describes gentle home exercise in comfortable surroundings. My assessment: making movement inviting may help reluctant exercisers get started, although the trend itself hasn’t established superior outcomes. Build from that starting point toward appropriate aerobic intensity and strength training. Current coverage · WHO activity guidance
Today’s trainer takeaway: Make exercise easy to start, then progress it; evaluate supplements by the specific formulation, population and outcome studied.
