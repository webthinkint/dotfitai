Neal, your November 1 roundup has a supplement safety alert and two new papers.
Ashwagandha recall: check the lot number — Oct. 30. Vitality Works recalled 365 Whole Foods Market Standardized + Full Spectrum Blend Ashwagandha, 120-count, lot 2025-26227, for potential Salmonella contamination. No illnesses were reported in the notice. Anyone with this lot should stop using it and discard it, rather than return it; refunds are available through Whole Foods. This is a specific product/lot recall. FDA notice
Plant-based diets: the replacement foods matter — Oct. 31. A study of 235 Swedish people aged 16–24 found fortified dairy alternatives contributed substantially to vitamin D, B12 and calcium intake in vegan diets. Some food-only nutrient intakes were nevertheless lower in vegan/vegetarian groups, while fiber was higher and saturated fat lower. This was observational, based on dietary recalls, and excluded supplements from the main analysis: lower food intake does not establish deficiency. Trainer takeaway: assess actual foods and fortification labels, not just the diet’s name. Original study
Healthy aging: muscle size doesn’t capture the whole strength picture — Oct. 31. In a comparison of 22 older and 18 younger men, the older group had 39% lower maximal leg-extension strength and roughly 49–55% lower rapid force development, despite no statistically significant difference in estimated thigh size. This small, cross-sectional study cannot show an individual’s aging trajectory or prove a training effect. Trainer takeaway: track strength and functional performance alongside body composition. Original study

Neal, a shorter November 2 roundup today: one new nutrition trial and one current fitness headline worth checking.
Monk-fruit blends: useful sugar-replacement evidence, with tolerance caveats. Published October 31, a randomized crossover trial in 30 healthy adults found that three monk-fruit blends containing fructooligosaccharides produced lower overall glucose and insulin responses than sugar during three-hour testing. Appetite and food intake did not differ, and mild diarrhea was more frequent with the blends. This supports these specific formulations as alternatives to sugar; it does not establish long-term weight-loss benefits or apply automatically to every monk-fruit product. Assessment is based on the publisher’s abstract; full text was paywalled. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Consider the complete sweetener blend and digestive tolerance, not just the “natural” label. Original study
Online claim check: the “best workout after 55” framing is overhyped; the exercise findings are useful. An October 27 headline says “Not walking or strength training,” highlighting sensorimotor training. The underlying study was published May 29, 2025, not newly released. Across 153 adults and 24 weeks, sensorimotor and aquatic programs improved several physical-function measures. However, there was no dedicated walking or conventional resistance-training comparison group, so the study cannot establish superiority over those activities. Small, uneven groups and predominantly female participants also limit generalization. Trainer takeaway: Balance and coordination work can enrich older-adult programming; this paper gives no reason to replace walking or progressive strength training. Current coverage · Original study

Neal, your November 3 roundup covers stimulant safety, vitamin D performance evidence, and a familiar weight-loss fad receiving fresh attention.
Energy drinks and stimulant supplements: a new safety signal, not a measured risk estimate. A November 2 American Heart Association release describes an analysis of over 28,000 FDA adverse-event reports. Cardiac arrest was disproportionately reported with caffeine/stimulant products. The widely quoted “11 times” figure concerns reporting odds, not an 11-fold increase in an individual consumer’s risk. These reports cannot establish causation, and the conference abstract has not yet undergone full journal peer review. Trainer takeaway: Review total caffeine and other stimulants across drinks and pre-workouts, especially when clients combine products. This does not establish that every caffeinated product carries the same risk. AHA research summary
Vitamin D and athletic performance: some encouraging tests, uncertain overall benefit. An October 28 systematic review found four randomized trials in combat-sport athletes, with 99 analyzed observations. Selected strength, power and endurance measures improved, but many related measures did not. Evidence certainty was very low, and no trial tested competition performance. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Correcting low vitamin D supports nutritional adequacy; promising an additional performance boost goes beyond this evidence. This is a new review of existing human trials, not a new clinical trial. Original review
“Oatzempic”: useful oats, overhyped medication comparison. Cleveland Clinic’s November 3 explainer revisits TikTok claims about blended oats, water and citrus as an alternative to GLP-1 drugs. Oats provide fiber and may help fullness, but this drink has not demonstrated medication-equivalent weight loss and is not a nutritionally complete meal replacement. The fresh coverage confirms continued discussion, not a measured new surge in popularity. Trainer takeaway: Welcome nutritious foods while protecting adequate protein and overall nutrition; don’t present an oat drink as a substitute for prescribed treatment. Current expert assessment
Neal, your November 4 roundup has a fasting comparison, a useful check on training metrics, and a muscle-health trend relevant to dotFIT.
5:2 versus 16:8 fasting: both worked over four weeks, with important limits. An October 28 randomized trial in 68 women found greater weight loss with 5:2 than 16:8: about 3.1 versus 2.2 kg. Both groups reduced calories; the 5:2 group received prepared, roughly 800-calorie meals for its two restricted days. There was no calorie-matched nonfasting group, so this cannot isolate a special fasting effect. Estimated muscle mass fell similarly in both groups, but bioimpedance cannot establish how much was actual muscle tissue versus fluid-related change. Trainer takeaway: Choose a sustainable eating pattern and monitor strength and nutritional adequacy alongside weight. Four weeks does not establish long-term superiority. Original trial
Training-load numbers: useful measurements, incomplete recovery signals. An October 31 systematic review of 46 studies found that most external-load metrics were better supported as measures of movement or work than as direct measures of fatigue, tissue stress or adaptation. Studies were generally small and predominantly male, with methods too varied for a pooled analysis. Trainer takeaway: Use workload data to inform coaching, then check perceived effort, symptoms and performance. A precise device reading does not automatically mean a precise estimate of recovery. Original review
Trend watch: the “Muscle Economy” is a promising direction, with product-specific caveats. November 3 coverage of Holland & Barrett’s 2027 forecast highlights growing interest in muscle preservation, protein, creatine and resistance training, partly alongside GLP-1 use. This is a retailer’s market assessment, not a clinical trial or proof that every related product works. dotFIT takeaway: It creates an opening to discuss strength and independence beyond bodybuilding. For medication-assisted weight loss, the established clinical emphasis remains adequate nutrition plus resistance exercise, rather than protein products alone. Current trend report · Joint clinical advisory
Neal, your November 5 roundup covers a supplement trial, reactive-balance training, and vibration-plate claims.
Multi-ingredient supplement: promising vascular results, not proof of longer life. The final NOVOS Core trial report was published November 4, following earlier disclosures. In a six-month randomized, blinded trial of 61 adults over 40, the formulation improved artery dilation by 2.9 percentage points and lowered systolic blood pressure by about 6 mmHg versus control. Only 43 participants completed the trial. It was small, company-supported, and measured biomarkers rather than heart attacks or longevity. dotFIT takeaway: Formulation-specific human testing is valuable, but these results cannot identify which ingredients worked or validate broader anti-aging claims. Original trial
Practicing balance recovery: encouraging early findings. A November 4 study followed 30 older adults through eight supervised treadmill sessions that deliberately challenged balance. Sit-to-stand performance, mobility and trip tolerance improved; slip tolerance did not significantly improve. There was no control group, so practice effects and other explanations remain possible, and fewer real-world falls were not established. Trainer takeaway: Reactive-balance work deserves consideration alongside strength training, but this protocol used specialist equipment and a safety harness. Don’t improvise unexpected slips or trips on an ordinary treadmill. Original study
Vibration plates: mixed evidence, overhyped as a shortcut. October 12 reporting documented TikTok enthusiasm; Cleveland Clinic added a fresh expert assessment today. There is some evidence for selected strength and balance benefits, but lasting advantages over conventional exercise and optimal protocols remain uncertain. Trainer takeaway: An optional, appropriately supervised addition to exercise, not a replacement for progressive strength training or a dependable stand-and-lose-fat solution. People with balance problems, injuries or relevant medical conditions should get individualized guidance first. Trend coverage · November 5 expert assessment
Neal, your November 6 roundup includes two new studies and a useful fitness-community trend.
Lighter weights can build muscle in beginners. A November 5 randomized trial had 22 young, untrained men complete six weeks of knee extensions with lighter or heavier loads, both taken to failure. Both groups gained quadriceps size and estimated strength, without a significant difference between groups. Lighter loading required more repetitions and total work. The small, short, single-exercise study does not establish equivalence for experienced lifters or prove that failure is necessary. Trainer takeaway: Lighter loading can be a practical option when heavier weights are unsuitable, provided the exercise remains sufficiently challenging. Original trial
Fermented dairy: extra muscle-aging benefits remain uncertain. A November 4 analysis of two Irish cohorts, totaling 6,790 adults, found no consistent relationship between fermented dairy intake and physical function after adjustment. This was a cross-sectional observational study using dietary questionnaires, so it cannot establish benefit or harm. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Yogurt and cheese can contribute protein; don’t promise additional strength or anti-sarcopenia effects specifically because they are fermented. These findings do not invalidate dairy’s nutritional value. Original study
Trend watch: fitness as a social outlet looks promising for gym engagement. McKinsey’s November 3 report, surveying over 10,000 consumers across four countries, says 30% of respondents view fitness as a primary social outlet, rising to 42% among Gen Z. It highlights run clubs and team-based fitness races. This is self-reported market research, not proof that these formats improve health or retention. Practical opportunity: Test welcoming small-group challenges and social training sessions, then measure participation and repeat attendance rather than assuming popularity guarantees results. Original consumer research
