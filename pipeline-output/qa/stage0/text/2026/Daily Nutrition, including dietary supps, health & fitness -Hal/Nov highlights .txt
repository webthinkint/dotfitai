Ashwagandha recall: check the lot number — Oct. 30. Vitality Works recalled 365 Whole Foods Market Standardized + Full Spectrum Blend Ashwagandha, 120-count, lot 2025-26227, for potential Salmonella contamination. No illnesses were reported in the notice. Anyone with this lot should stop using it and discard it, rather than return it; refunds are available through Whole Foods. This is a specific product/lot recall. FDA notice
Plant-based diets: the replacement foods matter — Oct. 31. A study of 235 Swedish people aged 16–24 found fortified dairy alternatives contributed substantially to vitamin D, B12 and calcium intake in vegan diets. Some food-only nutrient intakes were nevertheless lower in vegan/vegetarian groups, while fiber was higher and saturated fat lower. This was observational, based on dietary recalls, and excluded supplements from the main analysis: lower food intake does not establish deficiency. Trainer takeaway: assess actual foods and fortification labels, not just the diet’s name. Original study
Healthy aging: muscle size doesn’t capture the whole strength picture — Oct. 31. In a comparison of 22 older and 18 younger men, the older group had 39% lower maximal leg-extension strength and roughly 49–55% lower rapid force development, despite no statistically significant difference in estimated thigh size. This small, cross-sectional study cannot show an individual’s aging trajectory or prove a training effect. Trainer takeaway: track strength and functional performance alongside body composition. Original study

Neal, a shorter November 2 roundup today: one new nutrition trial and one current fitness headline worth checking.
Monk-fruit blends: useful sugar-replacement evidence, with tolerance caveats. Published October 31, a randomized crossover trial in 30 healthy adults found that three monk-fruit blends containing fructooligosaccharides produced lower overall glucose and insulin responses than sugar during three-hour testing. Appetite and food intake did not differ, and mild diarrhea was more frequent with the blends. This supports these specific formulations as alternatives to sugar; it does not establish long-term weight-loss benefits or apply automatically to every monk-fruit product. Assessment is based on the publisher’s abstract; full text was paywalled. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Consider the complete sweetener blend and digestive tolerance, not just the “natural” label. Original study
Online claim check: the “best workout after 55” framing is overhyped; the exercise findings are useful. An October 27 headline says “Not walking or strength training,” highlighting sensorimotor training. The underlying study was published May 29, 2025, not newly released. Across 153 adults and 24 weeks, sensorimotor and aquatic programs improved several physical-function measures. However, there was no dedicated walking or conventional resistance-training comparison group, so the study cannot establish superiority over those activities. Small, uneven groups and predominantly female participants also limit generalization. Trainer takeaway: Balance and coordination work can enrich older-adult programming; this paper gives no reason to replace walking or progressive strength training. Current coverage · Original study

Neal, your November 3 roundup covers stimulant safety, vitamin D performance evidence, and a familiar weight-loss fad receiving fresh attention.
Energy drinks and stimulant supplements: a new safety signal, not a measured risk estimate. A November 2 American Heart Association release describes an analysis of over 28,000 FDA adverse-event reports. Cardiac arrest was disproportionately reported with caffeine/stimulant products. The widely quoted “11 times” figure concerns reporting odds, not an 11-fold increase in an individual consumer’s risk. These reports cannot establish causation, and the conference abstract has not yet undergone full journal peer review. Trainer takeaway: Review total caffeine and other stimulants across drinks and pre-workouts, especially when clients combine products. This does not establish that every caffeinated product carries the same risk. AHA research summary
Vitamin D and athletic performance: some encouraging tests, uncertain overall benefit. An October 28 systematic review found four randomized trials in combat-sport athletes, with 99 analyzed observations. Selected strength, power and endurance measures improved, but many related measures did not. Evidence certainty was very low, and no trial tested competition performance. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Correcting low vitamin D supports nutritional adequacy; promising an additional performance boost goes beyond this evidence. This is a new review of existing human trials, not a new clinical trial. Original review
“Oatzempic”: useful oats, overhyped medication comparison. Cleveland Clinic’s November 3 explainer revisits TikTok claims about blended oats, water and citrus as an alternative to GLP-1 drugs. Oats provide fiber and may help fullness, but this drink has not demonstrated medication-equivalent weight loss and is not a nutritionally complete meal replacement. The fresh coverage confirms continued discussion, not a measured new surge in popularity. Trainer takeaway: Welcome nutritious foods while protecting adequate protein and overall nutrition; don’t present an oat drink as a substitute for prescribed treatment. Current expert assessment
Neal, your November 4 roundup has a fasting comparison, a useful check on training metrics, and a muscle-health trend relevant to dotFIT.
5:2 versus 16:8 fasting: both worked over four weeks, with important limits. An October 28 randomized trial in 68 women found greater weight loss with 5:2 than 16:8: about 3.1 versus 2.2 kg. Both groups reduced calories; the 5:2 group received prepared, roughly 800-calorie meals for its two restricted days. There was no calorie-matched nonfasting group, so this cannot isolate a special fasting effect. Estimated muscle mass fell similarly in both groups, but bioimpedance cannot establish how much was actual muscle tissue versus fluid-related change. Trainer takeaway: Choose a sustainable eating pattern and monitor strength and nutritional adequacy alongside weight. Four weeks does not establish long-term superiority. Original trial
Training-load numbers: useful measurements, incomplete recovery signals. An October 31 systematic review of 46 studies found that most external-load metrics were better supported as measures of movement or work than as direct measures of fatigue, tissue stress or adaptation. Studies were generally small and predominantly male, with methods too varied for a pooled analysis. Trainer takeaway: Use workload data to inform coaching, then check perceived effort, symptoms and performance. A precise device reading does not automatically mean a precise estimate of recovery. Original review
Trend watch: the “Muscle Economy” is a promising direction, with product-specific caveats. November 3 coverage of Holland & Barrett’s 2027 forecast highlights growing interest in muscle preservation, protein, creatine and resistance training, partly alongside GLP-1 use. This is a retailer’s market assessment, not a clinical trial or proof that every related product works. dotFIT takeaway: It creates an opening to discuss strength and independence beyond bodybuilding. For medication-assisted weight loss, the established clinical emphasis remains adequate nutrition plus resistance exercise, rather than protein products alone. Current trend report · Joint clinical advisory
Neal, your November 5 roundup covers a supplement trial, reactive-balance training, and vibration-plate claims.
Multi-ingredient supplement: promising vascular results, not proof of longer life. The final NOVOS Core trial report was published November 4, following earlier disclosures. In a six-month randomized, blinded trial of 61 adults over 40, the formulation improved artery dilation by 2.9 percentage points and lowered systolic blood pressure by about 6 mmHg versus control. Only 43 participants completed the trial. It was small, company-supported, and measured biomarkers rather than heart attacks or longevity. dotFIT takeaway: Formulation-specific human testing is valuable, but these results cannot identify which ingredients worked or validate broader anti-aging claims. Original trial
Practicing balance recovery: encouraging early findings. A November 4 study followed 30 older adults through eight supervised treadmill sessions that deliberately challenged balance. Sit-to-stand performance, mobility and trip tolerance improved; slip tolerance did not significantly improve. There was no control group, so practice effects and other explanations remain possible, and fewer real-world falls were not established. Trainer takeaway: Reactive-balance work deserves consideration alongside strength training, but this protocol used specialist equipment and a safety harness. Don’t improvise unexpected slips or trips on an ordinary treadmill. Original study
Vibration plates: mixed evidence, overhyped as a shortcut. October 12 reporting documented TikTok enthusiasm; Cleveland Clinic added a fresh expert assessment today. There is some evidence for selected strength and balance benefits, but lasting advantages over conventional exercise and optimal protocols remain uncertain. Trainer takeaway: An optional, appropriately supervised addition to exercise, not a replacement for progressive strength training or a dependable stand-and-lose-fat solution. People with balance problems, injuries or relevant medical conditions should get individualized guidance first. Trend coverage · November 5 expert assessment
Neal, your November 6 roundup includes two new studies and a useful fitness-community trend.
Lighter weights can build muscle in beginners. A November 5 randomized trial had 22 young, untrained men complete six weeks of knee extensions with lighter or heavier loads, both taken to failure. Both groups gained quadriceps size and estimated strength, without a significant difference between groups. Lighter loading required more repetitions and total work. The small, short, single-exercise study does not establish equivalence for experienced lifters or prove that failure is necessary. Trainer takeaway: Lighter loading can be a practical option when heavier weights are unsuitable, provided the exercise remains sufficiently challenging. Original trial
Fermented dairy: extra muscle-aging benefits remain uncertain. A November 4 analysis of two Irish cohorts, totaling 6,790 adults, found no consistent relationship between fermented dairy intake and physical function after adjustment. This was a cross-sectional observational study using dietary questionnaires, so it cannot establish benefit or harm. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Yogurt and cheese can contribute protein; don’t promise additional strength or anti-sarcopenia effects specifically because they are fermented. These findings do not invalidate dairy’s nutritional value. Original study
Trend watch: fitness as a social outlet looks promising for gym engagement. McKinsey’s November 3 report, surveying over 10,000 consumers across four countries, says 30% of respondents view fitness as a primary social outlet, rising to 42% among Gen Z. It highlights run clubs and team-based fitness races. This is self-reported market research, not proof that these formats improve health or retention. Practical opportunity: Test welcoming small-group challenges and social training sessions, then measure participation and repeat attendance rather than assuming popularity guarantees results. Original consumer research
Neal, here’s your November 7 fitness science roundup:
Probiotic plus iron: an encouraging but specific finding. A November 6 trial assigned 152 iron-deficient women without anemia to three groups for 12 weeks. The group receiving a particular probiotic strain with iron and vitamin C had a greater rise in ferritin, a marker of iron stores, than the iron-and-vitamin-C group. Hemoglobin and several other iron measures did not show a clear added benefit. The trial was short and commercially funded; it did not establish symptom relief or prove that other probiotics work the same way. Trainer takeaway: Don’t promise that a probiotic treats anemia or replaces an evaluation for low iron. Original study
Blood-flow restriction and aging: promising, but the extra benefit is limited. A November 3 randomized trial studied 36 very old adults with sarcopenia and mild cognitive impairment. After eight weeks, supervised low-load training with blood-flow restriction improved several measures compared with no exercise. But its added benefit over similar low-load exercise without restriction was limited, clearest for grip strength. This small study does not establish dementia prevention. Trainer takeaway: Accessible resistance training matters; specialized restriction protocols require appropriate screening and supervision. Original study
Fad watch: “fibermaxxing” has a sound core, but more isn’t automatically better. November 6 coverage shows the trend is still being discussed. A systematic review found health benefits associated with higher dietary fiber and improvements in some trial outcomes, but its observational disease findings do not prove causation, and it did not test fiber supplements. Trainer takeaway: Encourage fiber-rich foods and increase gradually with adequate fluids. Treating intake as a competition or assuming every gummy provides the same benefits is overhyped.
Neal, here’s your November 8 fitness science roundup:
Omega-3 and workout recovery: no advantage in a new trial. Published November 7, a randomized trial had 43 healthy adults ages 65–85 take an algae-derived omega-3 supplement or placebo for eight weeks before strenuous leg extensions. Omega-3 did not improve functional recovery. The study was small and tested one exercise challenge, so it does not settle omega-3’s other potential benefits or every recovery use. Trainer takeaway: Avoid promising faster recovery from omega-3 based on this evidence. Original study
Adding core work to knee rehab: encouraging, with a caveat. In a November 6 trial, 100 adults with mild-to-moderate knee osteoarthritis received knee rehabilitation, with or without added core training. At 12 weeks, activity pain and a mobility test favored the added-core group; knee strength improved similarly. The core group also had longer sessions, so the study cannot isolate core training from the extra exercise time. Trainer takeaway: Trunk exercises can be a useful addition to an individualized knee program, alongside appropriate clinical guidance. Original study
GLP-1s and muscle: a small laboratory clue, not a clinical verdict. Published November 4, researchers exposed human muscle cells from older adults to semaglutide or GIP. They found no major increase in the muscle-wasting signals tested, although semaglutide changed one measure of cellular energy production. These were cells in a lab, not people taking the drugs, and GIP is not tirzepatide. Trainer takeaway: The study does not rule out muscle loss during treatment. Adequate nutrition and resistance training remain important parts of weight-loss support. Original study
Trend watch: “cowmaxxing” and collagen: mixed evidence. November 8 Financial Times coverage describes demand for cattle-derived wellness products, including collagen and colostrum. That business reporting indicates interest, not proof of effectiveness. For skin-aging claims, a September reanalysis identified problems in influential collagen reviews and did not establish a benefit; other reviews have reported benefits but note low-quality evidence. Trainer/dotFIT takeaway: Discuss the specific formulation and intended use; don’t sell collagen as a proven rejuvenation shortcut.
Neal, Nov 9  roundup includes a particularly relevant new study for trainers supporting members on weight-loss medication.
Resistance training helped preserve lean tissue during tirzepatide treatment. In a randomized trial published November 9, 120 previously inactive adults ages 50–70 received tirzepatide; half also did supervised resistance training three times weekly for 40 weeks. Weight loss was similar at roughly 22 kg in both groups. The training group lost about 1.1 kg less lean soft tissue and gained strength, while strength declined without training. Lean soft tissue measured by DXA isn’t all muscle, and training reduced rather than eliminated its loss. The highly supervised program had excellent adherence, which may be harder to replicate. Trainer takeaway: This is strong, directly relevant support for pairing progressive resistance training with medication-assisted weight loss. Original study
A prebiotic changed gut bacteria without improving a liver marker. A November 4 pilot trial assigned 60 adults with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease to 1-kestose or placebo for eight weeks. Beneficial Bifidobacterium increased, but the primary liver-enzyme measure did not improve significantly versus placebo. The trial was small and short, so it cannot rule out other effects. dotFIT takeaway: A measurable microbiome change alone does not establish a meaningful health benefit. Original study
Trend watch: creatine gummies get a celebrity push. A November 4 feature highlights growing attention to gummies among women. Creatine itself has good evidence for repeated high-intensity exercise and resistance training; the gummy format has not been shown superior to powder. A small gummy study exists, but lacked a placebo or powder comparison. Trainer takeaway: Gummies may offer convenience. Compare the actual creatine dose, quality testing and cost rather than assuming a new format works better. NIH evidence summary
Neal, here’s your November 10 fitness science roundup:
Semaglutide and heart failure: new insight into how it may help. A November 8 randomized trial followed 81 adults with obesity and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction for 52 weeks. Semaglutide lowered heart-filling pressure during light exercise compared with placebo. More participants also reached normal measured pressures at rest and during exercise. This was a small, single-center study of heart physiology, not proof of a cure or longer survival. Trainer takeaway: Members with heart failure need an individually guided exercise plan; these results don’t establish benefits for healthy exercisers. Original study
Intervals versus steady cardio in prediabetes: both helped. Published November 10, a 12-week trial assigned 64 adults with prediabetes to interval or continuous training three times weekly. Intervals produced greater reductions in several body-fat measures, while both groups improved aerobic fitness and HDL without a clear difference between them. The study was small, tested multiple outcomes and had borderline statistical results for the fat measures. Trainer takeaway: Both approaches are useful options; choose one the member can safely sustain. Original study
Omega-3 and blood pressure: preliminary signal. A November 9 pilot trial of 100 adults already treated for high blood pressure and cholesterol found lower daytime systolic pressure after 12 weeks of a pharmaceutical omega-3 ethyl-ester product versus placebo. The finding is exploratory, with missing data and multiple comparisons, and needs replication. It did not establish fewer heart attacks or strokes. dotFIT takeaway: Interesting formulation-specific research, but not a basis to promise the same blood-pressure effect from every omega-3 product or replace prescribed treatment. Original study
Trend watch: cold plunges at wellness gatherings. November 8 coverage describes college wellness events that combine exercise and social activities; cold plunges remain a broader fitness trend. Evidence is mixed: cold-water immersion may reduce soreness, but certainty is low, and repeatedly using it immediately after lifting may blunt muscle-building adaptations. Trainer takeaway: Match recovery choices to the goal. People with heart or circulation conditions should seek medical advice before plunging. Research review
Neal, here’s your November 11 roundup. It’s a quieter day for major new fitness findings, but two studies offer useful perspective on personalized health claims.
Could genetics help explain different GLP-1 responses? A study published November 11 examined records of 13,725 adults starting semaglutide or tirzepatide. A small group with rare variants in the GLP-1 receptor gene lost less weight on semaglutide than matched non-carriers. The tirzepatide difference was not statistically significant. Only 33 people carried the qualifying variants, treatment was not randomized, and an independent analysis was not statistically conclusive. Trainer takeaway: Individual responses vary, but this early finding does not justify routine genetic testing or changing medication. Original study
Blood NAD levels and aging: the simple story is questionable. A November 4 analysis of more than 1,000 adults found that blood NAD levels did not generally decline with age in healthy men and rose modestly with age in healthy women. Lower levels were associated with cardiovascular disease, especially hypertension. The study was largely cross-sectional, so it cannot establish cause, and blood levels may not reflect muscle or brain levels. Some authors disclosed ties to NAD-testing businesses. dotFIT takeaway: A blood NAD reading is not established as a universal measure of biological aging, and this study does not show that raising it with supplements improves health. Original study
Tailored groceries: no clear added benefit in a small pilot. A November 6 randomized study of 210 adults with cardiometabolic conditions compared usual food-pantry support with medically tailored groceries, with or without coaching. All groups improved, but the tailored programs did not clearly outperform usual support on the exploratory measures. Four months of follow-up, self-reported outcomes and incomplete follow-up limit conclusions. Trainer takeaway: Food support can be valuable, but this study does not establish an advantage for the specific tailored program tested. Original study

Neal, here’s your November 12 fitness science roundup:
Hyperbaric oxygen and fitness: interesting association, not proof. A November 11 report reviewed 1,032 selected patients whose measured aerobic capacity was higher after roughly 59 hyperbaric oxygen sessions. There was no untreated comparison group, and exercise, rehabilitation and medication changes were not systematically tracked. One author disclosed a financial connection to an HBOT company. Trainer takeaway: This does not establish that HBOT caused the improvement or can replace training. Original study
Vitamin D raised blood levels, but performance gains were unclear. In a small randomized trial published November 11, 20 female handball players took vitamin D or placebo for eight weeks. Blood vitamin D increased with supplementation, but performance differences did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for multiple tests. The small sample cannot rule out a useful effect in other circumstances. Trainer takeaway: Address vitamin D needs without promising a performance boost to everyone. Original study
Trend watch: cream of rice as pre-workout fuel. November 11 coverage highlights renewed fitness-community interest. It can be a convenient, easily tolerated carbohydrate source, but the superfood framing is overhyped: research on carbohydrate fueling does not establish cream of rice as uniquely muscle-building or superior to other suitable foods. Trainer takeaway: Match pre-workout carbs to the session and the person’s digestive comfort, while meeting protein and fiber needs across the day. Carbohydrate research review
