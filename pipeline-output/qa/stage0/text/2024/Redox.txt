Redox homeostasis is a dynamic process that regulates the balance between reducing and oxidizing reactions within cells and plays a crucial role in various biological processes, including cellular signaling, development, and disease. ​
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are strong oxidants that are generated in aerobic organisms as a result of utilizing oxygen in metabolic processes. ​ ROS can act as secondary messengers and regulate signaling cascades by oxidizing proteins and altering DNA. ​
Redox regulation is essential for development in both plants and mammals. ​ In plants, redox signaling controls cell growth, division, and differentiation, while in mammals, redox regulation is involved in processes such as ATP production, oocyte maturation, and embryonic development. ​
ROS and oxidative stress are implicated in various human diseases, including inflammatory diseases, renal disease, and cancer. ​ However, the role of antioxidants in disease prevention and treatment is complex and context-dependent, with both beneficial and deleterious effects reported in different studies. ​
Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants have emerged as a potential strategy for disease treatment by specifically targeting ROS production in mitochondria. ​ However, the effects of these compounds on cancer progression have been diverse and sometimes unexpected, highlighting the complexity of redox regulation in disease. ​

Redox homeostasis refers to the balance between reducing (reduction) and oxidizing (oxidation) reactions within cells. ​ It is a dynamic process that ensures the stability of the redox status, or the balance between oxidized and reduced molecules, within cells. ​ Redox homeostasis is crucial for maintaining cellular function and preventing oxidative damage. ​
Redox homeostasis is maintained through a highly responsive system that senses changes in the redox status and adjusts metabolic activities to restore balance. ​ This system includes various antioxidant defense mechanisms and redox-regulating enzymes. ​ Some of the key components involved in maintaining redox homeostasis are: ​
Glutathione (GSH): GSH is a major antioxidant molecule that plays a critical role in maintaining redox balance. It acts as a reducing agent and can neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) by donating electrons. ​ GSH also participates in the regeneration of other antioxidants, such as vitamin C and vitamin E.
Thioredoxin (Trx) system: The Trx system consists of thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase enzymes. Thioredoxin acts as a reducing agent by transferring electrons to target proteins, thereby regulating their activity. Thioredoxin reductase regenerates reduced thioredoxin by using NADPH as a cofactor.
NADPH-regenerating systems: NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) is an essential cofactor for many redox reactions. NADPH-regenerating systems, such as the pentose phosphate pathway and the malic enzyme pathway, generate NADPH to support the activity of antioxidant enzymes and maintain redox balance. ​
Antioxidant enzymes: Various antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, play a crucial role in neutralizing ROS and preventing oxidative damage. These enzymes catalyze reactions that convert harmful ROS into less reactive or harmless molecules.
Transcription factors: Transcription factors, such as nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), regulate the expression of genes involved in antioxidant defense and redox regulation. Nrf2 activates the expression of antioxidant enzymes and other cytoprotective genes by binding to antioxidant response elements (AREs) in their promoter regions. ​
Overall, redox homeostasis is maintained by a complex interplay of antioxidant molecules, redox-regulating enzymes, and transcriptional regulation. ​ Disruption of this balance can lead to oxidative stress and contribute to the development of various diseases.
